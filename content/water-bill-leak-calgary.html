Short answer: if your Calgary water bill jumped and your habits didn't, you almost certainly have a leak you can't hear. The City says a slow leak that takes a minute to fill a 500 ml pop bottle adds over twenty cubic metres a month. At 2026 City rates that works out to roughly $72.92 a month, or about $875 a year — and the most common culprit is a toilet flapper that costs a few dollars and makes no noise at all.

That $875 figure is not a number The City publishes. It's the City's own leak benchmark multiplied by the City's own 2026 rates, and we're showing the arithmetic below so you can check it yourself rather than take our word for it.

One honest caveat on our own number: it assumes the leaked water also gets billed as wastewater volume, which is the case for an indoor leak going down a drain — a running toilet, a dripping tap. Water that passes the meter but never enters the drainage system, such as an outdoor tap or an irrigation line, is billed for water only. If you want to be conservative, the water-only cost is 20 × $1.7409 = $34.82 a month, about $418 a year. Even the conservative version is worth an afternoon of your time.

A leak on the buried service line outside the house is a different case again. In Calgary the meter sits inside the house, so that water never passes through it — a service line leak generally doesn't show on your bill at all. We cover how you actually find one at the bottom of this page.

And that's one slow leak. A toilet with a failed flapper can run considerably faster than a bottle a minute.

Check your meter — it takes ten minutes and costs nothing

Before you go looking for a leak, confirm you have one. Your water meter is the only impartial witness in the house.

  1. Turn off every water fixture in the house. Taps off, dishwasher and washing machine off, nobody flushing, ice maker off if you can, humidifier off, irrigation controller off. If you have a softener, note the time — you don't want it regenerating mid-test.
  2. Find the meter. In most Calgary homes it's in the basement, on the wall facing the street, usually near where the service line enters and often close to the hot water tank.
  3. Watch the flow indicator. Most meters have a small dial, triangle or star that spins on any flow at all. With everything off it should be dead still. If it's creeping, water is moving somewhere.
  4. Or take a reading and wait. If your meter has no obvious flow indicator, write down the reading, leave the house for two hours with everything off, and read it again. Any change is consumption you didn't cause.
  5. Know what the meter can and can't see. In Calgary the meter sits inside the house, so everything it registers is downstream of it — toilets, taps and fixtures, interior piping, the softener, the humidifier, the irrigation line. That's the leak a high bill is pointing at, and it's the one the rest of this page helps you find. Water escaping from the buried service line between the property line and the house is upstream of the meter: it never passes through, so it won't move the flow indicator and it won't inflate your consumption charge. That leak is real, but you find it a different way — covered at the bottom of this page.
  6. Narrow it down inside the house. If you can find the main shut-off valve just past the meter, close it and repeat the test. If the flow indicator stops, the leak is somewhere in the house past that valve and you can start room by room. If it keeps moving, the leak is in the short run between the meter and the shut-off itself.
  7. Do not touch the curb stop at the property line. That valve belongs to The City and homeowners and plumbers are not permitted to operate it. Breaking one turns a plumbing problem into a dig in your boulevard.

Sources: City of Calgary — water leaks and basic repairs; City of Calgary — water service lines.

The toilet dye test: start here, because it's usually the toilet

A silent toilet leak is the single most common cause of a mystery water bill, and it's the cheapest thing in your house to test. The flapper at the bottom of the tank is a rubber seal that hardens with age and with Calgary's hard water. Once it stops sealing, tank water trickles into the bowl continuously and drains away without a sound. Nothing looks wrong. The bill just goes up.

  1. Take the lid off the tank and put it somewhere it won't get knocked off.
  2. Drop in a few drops of food colouring — or a dye tablet if you have one. Any strong colour works.
  3. Don't flush. Wait 15 to 30 minutes. Go do something else.
  4. Look in the bowl. Colour in the bowl means the flapper is leaking, full stop. No colour means that toilet is fine — go test the next one.
  5. Flush to clear the dye so it doesn't stain.

Test every toilet in the house, including the basement one nobody uses. The unused ones are often the worst offenders, because a flapper that never gets exercised dries out and warps. A flapper is a cheap part and a straightforward swap. If the fill valve is also hissing or the water level is running over the top of the overflow tube, that's a second, separate problem — and if the toilet is old enough that parts are getting hard to find, replacing it usually pays for itself in water.

If it isn't the toilet, work down this list

  • A water softener regenerating too often. Softeners flush themselves with water on a schedule or by volume, and Calgary's hardness means they cycle more than the manual's default assumes. A softener stuck in regeneration, or set to regenerate nightly when it doesn't need to, can move a startling amount of water quietly into your floor drain. Our hard water page covers what our water actually measures and whether you need one at all.
  • A furnace humidifier. The bypass humidifiers fitted to a lot of Calgary furnaces drain continuously by design, and a stuck solenoid or a scaled-up pad makes that worse. If yours is still running in July, something's wrong.
  • Irrigation. An underground sprinkler system with a cracked line or a stuck valve leaks into the ground where you'll never see it. So does a controller that's still watering on a schedule you forgot about. If your bill climbed in May and never came back down, start here.
  • An outdoor tap. A frost-free sillcock that split over winter often leaks inside the wall only while the tap is open — you'll see nothing outside. Related: our outdoor tap page.
  • A dripping tap or a weeping shut-off. Less dramatic than it sounds financially, but easy to fix and worth ruling out.
  • An underground service line leak. The one with no visible water at all. Covered below, because it comes with a deadline.

If pressure has also dropped off while the bill went up, read those two symptoms together — that combination points at the supply side rather than a fixture. Our low water pressure page works through it.

Sanity-check your bill against the 2026 rates

Before assuming a leak, make sure you understand what you're actually being charged. Calgary residential water bills have five components, and only two of them move with how much water you use.

So $54.28 of a typical bill is fixed charges that arrive whether you use a drop or not. Subtract those from your total and what's left is the part a leak inflates. If your consumption charges are running $70 above what they were this time last year, that's roughly the profile of the twenty-cubic-metre leak in the calculation at the top of this page.

Flat rate versus metered matters too. Some Calgary properties are still on flat-rate billing rather than a meter, and The City states flat-rate customers pay roughly 68% more than metered customers. If you're on flat rate, a leak doesn't change your bill at all — which sounds like good news and isn't, because it means the leak runs undetected until it does structural damage or shows up as a wet spot in the yard. It also means that if your usage is average or below, switching to a meter is likely to save you money on its own.

The part nobody wants to tell you

As of August 2026, The City of Calgary's water leaks page makes no mention of bill credits, leak forgiveness or adjustments for water lost to a leak. We looked, because it's the first thing people ask. Some utilities elsewhere run leak-adjustment programs; we found nothing published for Calgary.

Absence of a published program is not the same as a flat refusal, so if your bill spiked badly it costs you nothing to phone 311 and ask. But plan on the assumption that the water you lost is water you paid for. That's precisely why the ten-minute meter check above is worth doing at the first sign of an odd bill instead of the third.

Source: The City of Calgary — water leaks and basic repairs, reviewed August 2026.

When the leak is underground: the 15-day rule

A leak on your buried water service line — the pipe running from the property line to your house — is the one exception to everything above, and the trap is that it usually isn't a high water bill. Your meter is inside the house, so a leak out in the yard is upstream of it. That water never passes through the meter, so it doesn't register as consumption and it doesn't land on your bill. Which is precisely why this kind of leak can run for months or years before anybody notices it.

What gives it away is the ground, not the paperwork. Look for a patch between the house and the street that stays wet or soft when nothing has been watered, a strip of lawn that greens up early in spring or never freezes through the middle of winter, water surfacing on the boulevard, or a drop in pressure at the fixtures. With every tap in the house off, put your ear to the main shut-off where the line comes through the wall — a steady hiss or rush of running water with nothing running is a strong signal. If any of that fits, call 311: The City dispatches leak detection to confirm it.

Two things you need to know. First, that pipe is yours. The City owns from the water main to your property line; you own from the property line to the house, and you pay all repair costs on the private-property portion, with the water meter as the one exception. The property line typically sits only a few metres in from the curb, so a leak under your boulevard grass can still be entirely your bill. We go through exactly where that boundary falls, and how to prove it, on our who-owns-which-pipe page.

Second, once a service line leak is confirmed, the clock starts. Under the Water Utility Bylaw the owner must repair it within 15 days, or The City may issue a remedial order. Repair methods are a trenchless service pull, a full excavation, or a hydrovac spot repair on a single defect — and The City publishes no cost figures for any of them. Fifteen days is enough time to get a second quote. It is not enough time to think it over for a fortnight first, so start phoning the day you get the letter.

Source: The City of Calgary — water service line leaks.
