You've got an accepted offer, a conditions deadline in about a week, and a home inspection booked. The question is whether to spend more money on a camera down the sewer line before the clock runs out.

Short answer: A standard home inspection does not include the buried sewer line — it's a visual, non-invasive inspection that stops at the visible piping. A sewer scope is a separate service, and it's worth booking on a pre-1970 Calgary home, on any house with mature boulevard trees, and on anything with a finished basement. Nobody publishes authoritative pricing for it here, but as a planning frame it's a flat fee in the territory of an hour or two of plumber time. Book it the day your offer is accepted, because it's the one condition that regularly runs out of runway.

This page exists because almost nothing written on it in Calgary connects the three things that actually matter together: what the camera finds, what fixing it costs here, and who ends up paying once possession happens. Home inspectors write about the first, and then it stops.

What your home inspection does and doesn't cover

Home inspectors do a genuinely useful job and this isn't a criticism of them. It's a scope-of-work issue. A standard inspection is visual and non-invasive: your inspector will run the fixtures, watch drains empty, look at the accessible piping in the basement and mechanical room, check the floor drain, and flag anything visible — a corroded stack, evidence of past water on the floor, poor slope on exposed drain runs.

What that can't tell anyone is the condition of the pipe between your foundation wall and the City main under the street, which is buried, unlit, and typically 10 to 20 metres long. There is exactly one way to see it: put a camera down it.

Ask this question when you book your inspection, in these words: "Does your inspection include a camera inspection of the main sewer line to the street, and if so does it include recorded footage, a distance log and a written report?" Some Calgary inspectors offer it as an add-on and some subcontract it. If the answer is no or vague, book a plumber separately — and try to get both on site the same day so you're not spending two of your condition days on scheduling.

This city's housing stock came in waves, and each wave has a sewer signature. That makes the decision less of a coin flip than it looks.

What the camera actually shows — and what it can't

Pipe material and era

Clay tile, cast iron, ABS or PVC are all recognisable on camera, so you find out what you're buying rather than inferring it from the year of construction — which is often wrong, because lines get partially replaced.

Root intrusion, and how bad

You see whether it's fine hairs at one joint or a mass occupying most of the bore, which is the difference between a maintenance item and a repair you should be negotiating over.

Bellies, offsets and cracks

A sag holding standing water, a joint that's shifted, a longitudinal crack — all visible, and all things that tell you how the line will behave over the next decade rather than just today.

Distance to each defect

A proper scope logs how far down the line each finding sits, which is the number that decides whether a repair is on your side of the property line or The City's. Insist the distances are in the written report.

What it can't tell you

Depth of the pipe, exact soil conditions, or what's outside the pipe wall. Those drive excavation cost, so a scope tells you that there's a problem and roughly where — not precisely what the dig will cost.

Where it can't go

Cameras get stopped by hard blockages, tight bends and collapsed sections. Ironically, "couldn't complete the run" is itself an important finding — and it's a reason to ask for the footage rather than a summary.

One practical note: the camera needs a way in. Most Calgary homes have a cleanout in the basement near where the sewer exits the foundation. If there isn't an accessible one, the plumber may need to pull a toilet, which takes longer and needs the seller's permission. Flag it on the phone when you book so nobody wastes a trip you're paying for.

The Calgary rule that turns a finding into your bill

This is the part missing from every home-inspection page on this topic, and it's the part that decides the size of the number.

In Calgary, The City owns the water and sewer service lines from the mains under the street up to your property line. The homeowner owns the run from the property line to the house and pays all repair costs on that private-side portion. And the property line typically sits only a few metres in from the edge of the curb or sidewalk — not at the road.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service lines; see also Water and sewer connections. Current as of August 2026.

So when a scope report says "offset joint with root mass at 12 metres," the follow-up question is where the front property line falls. Pull the Real Property Report from the seller's disclosure package — it's a legal survey and it dimensions the parcel boundary. If the defect is inside the line, it's the buyer's problem the day possession happens. Nobody at The City is coming to fix it, and nobody is reimbursing you for it. Our full explainer on who owns which pipe, and how to prove it goes through the geometry properly.

What that means in a negotiation: a scope finding on the private side is a defect in the property you're buying, in the same category as a failing roof or a cracked heat exchanger. It's not an act of God and it's not the municipality's to sort out. That framing is what makes it negotiable.

Turning a finding into a number, then into a deal

  1. Get the footage and the written report, not a verbal. "The plumber said it looked rough" carries no weight with a seller's agent. A dated report with distances, a material identification and a still image does.
  2. Get a written repair quote inside the conditions period. This is the step most buyers skip and it's the one that decides the outcome. You want a plumber's fixed-price quote for the actual recommended repair — that becomes the number in the conversation instead of two agents trading guesses.
  3. Ask what method the quote assumes. A trenchless pull, a full excavation and a hydrovac spot repair on a single defect are very different jobs with very different prices and very different damage to the yard. A quote that doesn't say which one it is isn't finished.
  4. Decide what you're actually asking for. A price reduction, the seller completing the repair before possession, or a holdback in trust. Reduction is cleanest; seller-completed repairs need a permit condition and proof of inspection or you inherit unpermitted work.
  5. Talk to your lender and insurer if it's significant. A known defect can matter to both, and finding that out during conditions is the point of having conditions.
  6. Keep everything for the file. The report, the quote and the correspondence. If you buy the house and the line fails in three years, that file is what your insurer, and possibly your lawyer, will want.

On cost, we'll be straight rather than useful-sounding: there is no authoritative public source for Calgary plumbing prices, and every figure published online is a company's self-report. Sewer repair spans from a few hundred dollars for a clean and a re-scope to one of the most expensive single repairs a Calgary house can hand you — depth, length, surface above the line and access change it by orders of magnitude. That's why you get a written quote on the actual pipe rather than trusting a range from a web page, including ours. For hourly rates and how to read a quote, see what Calgary plumbers charge.

Timing it inside a conditions period

Calgary conditions periods commonly run about seven to ten days, sometimes five. Here's how the scope fits without eating the whole thing.

  • Book the day the offer is accepted. Not after the home inspection report lands. The scope and the inspection can run the same day, and often should.
  • Confirm access in writing through your agent. The two things that turn a booking into a wasted trip are an occupied house nobody warned, and no accessible cleanout.
  • Ask about the basement finish level up front. If the cleanout is behind drywall or under flooring, the plumber needs to know before they arrive.
  • Leave two days at the end. You need time to get a repair quote after the finding, and quotes take a day or two even when everyone's motivated.
  • If it's tight, ask for a short extension early. Asking on day two because you're waiting on a specialist reads as diligent. Asking on the last afternoon reads as disorganised, and sellers say no.

For agents

If you're representing buyers in Calgary's inner city, this is a cheap condition that prevents an expensive post-possession phone call. Three things are worth building into your process: ask sellers' side for the Real Property Report early so the property line is available when a distance reading appears; treat "recommend monitoring" as a real disclosure item rather than a pass; and know that the private-side rule means a boulevard-adjacent defect is your buyer's cost, not The City's, which is the piece most clients get wrong on their own.
