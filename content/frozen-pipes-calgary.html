Short answer: If water is coming out, shut off the main first — basement, wall facing the street, usually near the water heater — then cut the power to any wet area, then photograph everything before you touch it. If the pipe is frozen but still holding, open the tap it feeds and warm the pipe from the tap end backwards with a hair dryer. Never a torch, never an open flame.

Two completely different jobs, and mixing them up costs money. Pick your path and ignore the other one until the water is under control.

Path 1: the pipe has already burst

A half-inch supply line at household pressure moves a startling amount of water. The order below is deliberate.

  1. Shut off the main. Typically the street-facing basement wall, low down, near the meter and the hot water tank — behind a furnace room partition in an older bungalow, in an unfinished corner of the mechanical room in a 1980s build. Clockwise on a round handle; a quarter turn on a lever. If it's seized, and old gate valves here seize constantly, don't force it into snapping — move on and call.
  2. Kill the power to anything wet. Live circuits in a flooding basement is the part that can actually hurt you. Breakers off for the affected area. If the panel itself is in the wet zone, don't wade in — call the utility and stay out.
  3. Open the taps to drain the system. With the main off, open the lowest cold tap in the house and a couple upstairs. That drops the pressure and drains the water still sitting above the break, so it goes down a drain instead of through your ceiling.
  4. Photograph everything before you clean up. Wide shots of the room, close shots of the split, the water line on the wall, the soaked contents. Adjusters assess what they can see — a floor you've already mopped is worth nothing to your claim.
  5. Then start extracting water. Wet vac, towels, fans, contents up off the floor. Speed beats thoroughness — standing water wicks into drywall within hours.

Find your main shut-off before you need it. The most useful five minutes a Calgary homeowner can spend in October is walking down to the basement, finding that valve, and cycling it once to confirm it works. A valve you discover is seized while the ceiling is coming down is the difference between a wet floor and a claim.

One thing you cannot do: operate the curb stop at the property line. That valve belongs to The City and neither homeowners nor plumbers may touch it. If your interior main won't close, that's a 311 call alongside the plumber. We map where the ownership line falls separately.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service lines

Path 2: frozen, but it hasn't let go yet

The tell is localized: one fixture has no water or a weak dribble, everything else is normal. If nothing in the house has water, the freeze is more likely in the service line from the street — a 311 call, not a hair dryer job. If water goes in fine but won't go out and several fixtures back up at once, that's a frozen sewer line, a different problem with a different fix.

  1. Open the tap that pipe feeds first. The step people skip, and the one that matters most. Melting ice releases water, and that water needs an exit — an open tap gives it one and relieves the pressure that would otherwise build against the plug. Both hot and cold sides.
  2. Work from the tap end backwards. Heat closest to the open faucet first, then move gradually toward the frozen section. Heat the middle of the plug and meltwater gets trapped between two blocks of ice — which is exactly how a pipe that would have survived splits instead.
  3. Use gentle, boring heat. Hair dryer on low, a heat lamp at a safe distance, an electric heating pad wrapped round the pipe, or hot towels re-wrapped as they cool. Keep it moving. It's slow, and slow is correct.
  4. Warm the room, not just the pipe. Open the cabinet doors under the sink so household heat reaches the pipes behind them. A space heater aimed into the cavity — never left unattended — often does more than the hair dryer.
  5. Watch the pipe as flow returns. Stay there with a flashlight and look at the whole run. Freeze splits frequently don't leak until the ice clears and pressure comes back. A bulge, a hairline crack or a bead of water at a joint means shut the main off again and go to Path 1.
  6. Don't assume it's over. A pipe that froze once freezes in the same spot next cold snap, because the reason — missing insulation, an air leak, a run through unheated space — hasn't changed.

Never use an open flame. No propane torch, no blowtorch, no charcoal or kerosene heater. Two reasons, both bad: torching a pipe superheats water inside the ice plug and can rupture the line violently, and a flame inside a wall cavity sits directly against dry framing, old paper-faced insulation and years of dust — in a void you can't see into, can't monitor and can't reach with an extinguisher, so a smouldering start there has time to spread before you know it exists. If a pipe is inaccessible enough that you're reaching for a torch, that's the moment to call instead.

Call a plumber if you can't find the frozen section, if it's inside a wall you'd have to open up, if it's the main service line, or if you think it's already split. Our emergency page has an honest call-now versus call-Monday list.

Where Calgary pipes actually freeze

Rarely a random pipe in a heated room. The failures cluster where building heat doesn't reach:

  • Garage walls. A line run through a shared garage wall to feed a laundry sink, garage tap or the bathroom above. Unheated space, barely insulated cavity.
  • The outdoor tap run. The section between the interior shut-off and the hose bib is the classic Calgary burst, and it almost always traces back to a hose left on in the fall. The full winterizing sequence →
  • Rim joists and the sill plate area. Where the basement wall meets the floor above. Air leaks there let −30 °C air sit directly against a pipe that thinks it's indoors.
  • Crawl spaces and unheated additions. Converted porches and sunrooms over a crawl space with no heat register.
  • Vacant or lightly heated homes. Mid-renovation, between tenants, or thermostat turned down while the owners winter somewhere warmer.

So how long does it take for a pipe to freeze?

Nobody can give you a real number, and be sceptical of the pages that do — the internet's favourite "six hours" line gets repeated everywhere with no source behind it. What decides it is exposure, wind, insulation, pipe material and whether water is moving. A well-insulated pipe in a wall cavity can ride out a week at −25 °C; an uninsulated line in a windy crawl space can freeze overnight at −12 °C. A cold snap with bare ground is harder on plumbing than a colder one with a foot of snow, because snow is insulation.

The one number worth acting on comes from The City: keep the home at a minimum of 15 °C to protect the water service line, use only UL-listed heat tape, and disconnect garden hoses before winter. That figure is aimed squarely at empty houses set to 10 °C to save gas, which is where most of this city's frozen service lines come from.

Source: The City of Calgary — Frozen water lines

Who pays — and the water meter trap

Same split as any other water service issue. The City owns the pipe from the main to your property line; you own it from there to the house and pay all repair costs on that private portion. That line usually sits only a few metres in from the curb, so a freeze two metres on the wrong side of an invisible boundary is entirely your bill.

The exception homeowners rely on is the water meter, which The City normally owns. Not here: a frozen or damaged water meter is the homeowner's cost, stated plainly in the City's guidance. It catches people out every winter, because the meter sits in the coldest corner of a cold basement — another argument for the 15 °C rule and for leaving the utility room register open.

What happens when you call 311

If the frozen section looks like the service line into the house — no water anywhere in the building, deep cold snap — report it to 311. As of August 2026, the City describes the process this way:

  • The City may send a crew to confirm your service is actually frozen rather than taking the report at face value.
  • Response may be delayed during high volumes. In the week a cold snap breaks, everyone calls at once — plan around it.
  • You must sign a Work Authorization Form before the City will thaw the private-property portion. Read it first — it sets out what you're agreeing to.
  • At-risk properties get letters directing them to the Frozen Pipes Prevention Program. That's not junk mail — it means your address has a history or a known risk factor.

Source: The City of Calgary — Frozen water lines

We're deliberately not telling you what the City charges to thaw a service line, or what happens cost-wise if crews attend and the freeze turns out to be entirely on your side. Neither figure could be verified from a City source, and inventing one would be worse than saying so. Ask 311 when you report it — before the crew is dispatched, not after.

Insurance: the part people find out too late

Alberta burst-pipe claims get disputed over two things: whether the house was being checked, and whether the damage was sudden or the slow result of poor maintenance. An Alberta brokerage states that most insurers require a vacant home to be checked every 24 to 72 hours for burst-pipe damage to be covered, and that clogs and high water pressure are treated as maintenance and likely not covered. It also names the service line endorsement as the add-on covering underground service lines against tree roots, corrosion and shifting earth — coverage plenty of Calgary homeowners assume they already have and don't.

Source: ACERA Insurance — Are burst pipes covered by home insurance in Alberta?

That's one brokerage's description, not a universal rule, and not your policy. Two practical moves: read your own vacancy clause before you travel in January, and if the house will be empty, have a named person physically enter it on a schedule and keep a dated text log. Cheap evidence.
