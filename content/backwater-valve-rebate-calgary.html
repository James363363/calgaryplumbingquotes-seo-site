You're pricing a backwater valve, someone mentioned there's a rebate, and now you're trying to find it on calgary.ca and coming up empty. You're not missing a page.

Short answer: As of August 2026, there is no City of Calgary rebate, subsidy or grant for residential backwater valves or sump pumps. Calgary's only stormwater incentive is a developer program for projects with three or more primary housing units, and it doesn't cover either device. Edmonton homeowners can get up to $800 through EPCOR; Calgary homeowners currently get nothing. If you install one here, you pay for all of it.

Sources: The City of Calgary — Stormwater incentive program and Climate-ready measures: drainage and water management.

That's the whole answer, and it's the one nobody in this market seems willing to write down.

Why everyone assumes there is one

Because almost everywhere else, there is. Edmonton runs a backwater valve subsidy through EPCOR. Cities across Ontario have had flood-prevention grants for years. So when a Calgarian hears about a rebate from a sibling in Edmonton or a coworker from Toronto, the reasonable conclusion is that Calgary must have something similar and they just haven't found the page yet.

The second reason is less innocent. At least one Calgary plumbing company's page on this topic offers to "point you to the current program on calgary.ca" — language that implies a program exists without naming it, dating it, or linking it. Nobody said anything false. You still come away believing money is available and calling them to find out how to get it.

The test to apply to any plumber who mentions a rebate: ask them to email you the link. Not the offer to help with paperwork — the actual URL on calgary.ca, before you book. How a company handles that request tells you how they'll handle the parts of the quote you can't independently check either.

Calgary homeowners install them anyway. Here's why that's still rational

It's the one device that stops the main coming back at you

A backwater valve is a one-way gate on your building drain: waste flows out, and when the sanitary main surcharges, a flap closes against the reverse flow. Nothing else on your property does that job — which means on a house with a finished basement it's the difference between a wet afternoon and replacing drywall, flooring, and everything stored down there.

The code may already require protection

The National Plumbing Code 2020 requires fixtures located below the level of the adjoining street and subject to backflow to be protected (s. 2.4.6.4). In plain terms, a basement bathroom or laundry below street level generally needs backflow protection — so on a basement development this isn't a nice-to-have you're choosing, it's part of passing inspection.

A permit means it gets inspected

A plumbing permit is required to install a backflow prevention device. That's a benefit disguised as paperwork: a permitted install is inspected, so you find out it's the right device, the right way round and accessible for servicing now — not on the night the main surcharges and the flap doesn't seat.

Your insurer may care

Whether a valve changes your sewer backup coverage or your premium is specific to your policy and your insurer, so it's a phone call, not a rule. Make it before you book the work — if there's a discount attached, it partly offsets an install you're paying for entirely out of pocket.

Sources: The City of Calgary — Climate-ready measures (permit requirement) and the City's plumbing advisory on backwater valves (NPC 2020 s. 2.4.6.4).

What actually drives the cost — and why nobody will quote you on the phone

Every Calgary plumbing company refuses to publish a number for this, and for once they're right to. We're not going to publish one either, and here's the honest reason: the same device in two houses on the same street can differ by a multiple, because the work isn't the valve, it's getting to the drain.

  • Access to the main building drain. If the line runs under an open, unfinished basement floor with a cleanout nearby, it's a straightforward job. If it runs under a finished rec room, a tiled bathroom or a bearing wall, the flooring comes up first and goes back after.
  • How deep the pipe sits. A drain 300 mm under the slab and a drain a metre down are different excavations, different shoring considerations and different re-fill volumes — so depth alone can move the price more than the choice of valve.
  • Concrete cutting and making good. Interior installs mean saw-cutting the slab, digging out, setting the valve and access box, then re-pouring. Ask specifically whether the quote includes restoring the concrete to a finished surface, because "patched" and "finished" are different line items.
  • Interior versus exterior. An exterior install in the yard avoids cutting your floor but means excavation, and in Calgary that means frost depth and a season when it's practical.
  • Whether the line is fit for it. If the camera shows a cracked or root-filled clay line, you're not adding a valve to a healthy drain — there's a repair conversation first. Who owns which section of that pipe decides who pays for it.
  • Timing. Doing it as part of a basement development while the floor is already open costs a fraction of coming back for it as a standalone job in five years.

What to insist on instead of a phone number: a site visit, a written fixed price, the make and model of the valve, confirmation that the permit and inspection are included and in whose name, and a line covering the concrete. How to check whether you already have one

  1. Look at the basement floor near where the sewer exits. Usually the street-facing side, in the same neighbourhood as the floor drain and the cleanout. You're looking for a round or rectangular access cover set flush in the concrete, often plastic, sometimes with a clear lid.
  2. Don't confuse it with the cleanout or the floor drain. The cleanout is a capped pipe fitting for augering the line; the floor drain is a grate. A backwater valve has a service cover big enough to reach the flap through.
  3. Check the permit and inspection history. If you bought recently, your purchase file and Real Property Report may tell you. A permitted install leaves a record.
  4. Camera the line if it still isn't clear. A valve body is unmistakable from the inside, and you get a condition report on the rest of the drain at the same time — two answers for one visit.
  5. If you have one, service it. Open the cover once a year, check the flap is clean and moves freely, and clear any debris caught on the seat. An unmaintained valve that can't close is the same as not having one, and this is the maintenance almost everybody skips.
