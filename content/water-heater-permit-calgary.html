Short answer: Not always — and the sites telling you a permit is always required in Alberta are skipping a step. The City of Calgary does not require a plumbing permit to replace existing fixtures or repair a leak, and on the plumbing side a like-for-like tank swap is a fixture replacement. What usually does put a permit in play is the gas: the connection, the venting, or a change in the type of heater. That's a gas permit, and a Calgary homeowner cannot pull one.

Your tank is weeping onto the floor, or it's fifteen years old and you've decided not to gamble on another February. You want to know whether this turns into a paperwork exercise before you start phoning for prices. Here's the accurate version, with the City's own rules underneath it.

What the City of Calgary actually says

Two short lists do most of the work. The City states that a plumbing permit is required when a plumbing system is constructed, extended or altered, and when the water and sewer lines in or around the home are replaced. It also states that a plumbing permit is not required to repair a leak in a water distribution or drainage system, to replace existing faucets and fixtures, or to remove a blockage in the drainage.

Source: The City of Calgary — Homeowner electrical and plumbing permits (current as of August 2026)

You will find Calgary plumbing companies stating, without a citation, that "in Alberta you need a permit to replace a hot water tank." That's not what the City's page says, and the reason to care isn't pedantry — it's that a vague "you need a permit" is often doing the work of justifying a price. Ask which permit, for which part of the job. A legitimate contractor answers in one sentence.

So on the water side, a tank going back into the same location on the same supply and drain connections doesn't, by itself, trigger a plumbing permit. The honest complication is that on most Calgary houses the water side isn't the part that decides.

The four things that flip the answer to yes

Read the City's rules against what a water heater replacement physically involves, and four situations move the job from "fixture swap" into permitted territory. If none of these apply, you're generally out of the permit conversation. If any one does, you're in it.

1. The gas line changes

If the line feeding the appliance is moved, extended, re-routed or resized upstream of the tank, that's gas work — permitted, and done by a licensed contractor. Common on older inner-city homes where the existing line is undersized for anything modern.

2. The venting design or size changes

Different vent diameter, different material, a new termination, or moving from a shared masonry chimney to a dedicated vent. Venting is a combustion-safety matter, which is why it's the item inspectors look hardest at.

3. You change the type of heater

Tank to tankless, atmospheric to power-vent, gas to electric. A change of type is not a like-for-like replacement in any meaningful sense — the venting, the gas demand and often the water connections are all different.

4. Water or sewer lines get replaced as part of the job

Less common on a tank swap, but if the state of the surrounding pipe means you're replacing service lines while you're in there, the City calls that out specifically as permit work.

The practical translation for a typical Calgary basement: a straight, same-size, same-vent gas tank replacement is treated as a swap, but it still involves disconnecting and reconnecting a gas appliance — and that's the part you personally can't do.

The gas rule that decides most of these jobs

Homeowners in Calgary cannot take out a gas permit. The only exception is a homeowner who personally holds Alberta first- or second-class gasfitter certification, resides or intends to reside at the address, and applies in person with proof of qualifications. For everybody else, a licensed contractor pulls the permit and performs the gas work.

Sources: City of Calgary — Homeowner permits and City of Calgary — Plumbing and gas permits

This is why the DIY route on a gas tank collapses even though the province's owner exemption lets you do your own plumbing. Gasfitting is a separate trade certification from plumbing in Alberta — the two aren't interchangeable, and a plumbing ticket doesn't cover a gas connection. The full explanation of what you can and can't legally do yourself is here.

Benefit of getting this right rather than working around it: a permitted gas appliance gets a City inspector's eyes on the venting and the connection, which means a combustion or spillage problem gets caught by someone whose job it is to catch it, not by your family six months later.

Tank to tankless: the case where permits are certain

If you're switching to tankless, stop treating it as a water heater purchase and start treating it as a small renovation. Three things change at once:

  • Gas demand. A tankless unit fires at a far higher input than the 40- or 50-gallon tank it replaces. The existing branch line and sometimes the meter capacity need checking against everything else on the system — furnace, range, fireplace, garage heater — not just against the new unit in isolation.
  • Venting. The old vent is essentially never reusable. Different material, different sizing, a new exterior termination that has to clear windows, doors and walkways — and in Calgary, one that won't get buried by a snow drift or ice up during a chinook freeze-thaw.
  • Water and condensate. New connections, usually isolation valves for descaling, and a condensate drain on a condensing unit.

Descaling matters more here than in most Canadian cities. Calgary water runs roughly 141–200 mg/L of calcium carbonate from the Bearspaw plant and 181–274 mg/L from Glenmore — hard to very hard. Scale is what kills heat exchangers, so factor annual servicing into the tankless decision rather than discovering it in year three.

Fees and inspections, if a permit is in play

A homeowner plumbing permit is $116.50: $112 plus a $4.50 Safety Codes Council fee, with no processing fee. Fees rise annually on January 1. A contractor's trade permit is $112 processing plus $9.79 per $1,000 of construction value, with $116.50 as the minimum — on a water heater job that generally lands at or near the floor.

Sources: City of Calgary — Homeowner permits; City of Calgary building and trade permit fee schedule

That figure is worth carrying into a quote conversation. Permit cost is almost never what makes a water heater job expensive — labour, the unit itself, and any venting or gas modification are. Where an inspection applies, expect the standard sequence: work inspected before it's concealed, then a final once everything is in and operating. Someone 18 or older needs to be at the property, inspections are booked through 311 or the City's online system, and next-day requests generally need to be in by 2 p.m.

Why an unpermitted gas appliance is a problem later

Nothing happens for years. Then one of two things does.

You sell. A home inspector opens the utility room, sees a gas appliance, and there's no permit record behind it. That becomes a condition, a holdback, or a price adjustment — and the amount a buyer asks for is never $116.50. Unlike a hidden pipe in a wall, a water heater is sitting in plain sight with a date sticker on it.

You claim. If something goes wrong, your insurer will ask who did the work and whether it was inspected. One Alberta brokerage notes that damage treated as a maintenance issue is generally not covered, and points to a service line endorsement as the specific add-on for underground supply problems. Read your own policy and ask your broker directly rather than assuming either way.

Source: Acera Insurance — burst pipes and Alberta home insurance (brokerage commentary, not an insurer policy document)

Before you replace: is the tank actually the problem?

Worth thirty seconds, because replacement gets recommended a lot faster than it needs to be. No hot water at all is often a pilot, thermocouple, gas valve or — on electric — an element or thermostat, all of which are repairs rather than replacements. Water at the base of the tank can be condensation, a T&P discharge, or a fitting, not necessarily a rusted-through shell.
