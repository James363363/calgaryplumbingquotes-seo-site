Most people read this page on the worst possible day — the day a plumber hands them a quote with five figures on it and says the words "it's on your side." Here's what determines whether that's true.

Short answer: The City of Calgary owns the water and sewer service pipes from the mains under the street up to your property line. You own everything from the property line into your house, and you pay all repair costs on that private-side portion — the water meter is the single exception. Because the property line typically sits only a few metres in from the edge of the curb or sidewalk, a failure that looks like it's "out by the road" is very often still yours.

Sources: The City of Calgary — Water service lines and Water and sewer connections. As of August 2026.

Almost every page you'll find on this question is either written for Toronto or written by a plumbing company that never links a City of Calgary source. The rule here is specific, it's published, and it applies to both pipes — the water line coming in and the sewer line going out. Most competing pages only talk about the sewer, which is how people get blindsided by the other half.

Picture the geometry, because it's the whole argument

Stand on your front step and look toward the street. Under that ground there are two separate pipes running from your house out to the road, and they belong to different people at different points along their length.

  1. Inside your basement wall. Your water service enters through the foundation, usually on the street-facing side, and the first thing it hits is your main shut-off valve and then the water meter. Your sewer leaves through the foundation on roughly the same side, often with a cleanout — a capped access point — near where it exits.
  2. Under your front yard. Both pipes run out under your lawn, driveway or front garden. Every metre of this is yours: yours to maintain, yours to repair, yours to pay for.
  3. The property line. This is the boundary of the parcel you actually own. It is not the curb and it is not the sidewalk. In most Calgary neighbourhoods it lands a few metres back from the edge of the curb or sidewalk, which means the strip of grass you mow between your sidewalk and the road — the boulevard — is frequently City land while the pipe under part of it is still on your side of the line.
  4. Past the property line. From there out to the water main and the sanitary sewer main under the street, the pipe is The City's responsibility.

The practical version: your ownership does not stop at your fence, your hedge, or your sidewalk. It stops at a surveyed line that you cannot see from the street.

How to find your actual property line

Don't estimate it. The document you want is your Real Property Report — the legal survey most Calgary homeowners received when they bought, often filed with the mortgage paperwork. It shows the parcel boundary with dimensions from the house. If you can't find yours, an Alberta Land Surveyor can produce one, and your lawyer's file from the purchase may already have a copy.

Then get the defect located by distance. A camera inspection reports depth and distance from the cleanout — "root mass at 11.5 metres." Put that against the survey dimension to the front property line and the ownership question answers itself instead of coming down to whose guess sounds more confident.

Don't let anyone settle this verbally. If a contractor tells you a defect is on your side, ask for the camera footage and the distance reading in writing. If they tell you it's The City's side, that's a 311 call, not a repair. Either way the number matters more than the opinion.

The curb stop is The City's, and neither you nor your plumber may touch it

Somewhere near your property line there's a small round metal cap set into the lawn or the sidewalk. That's the access to your water service valve, usually called the curb stop — the shut-off for the water feeding your house from the main. It sits very close to the property line, but The City owns it, and homeowners and plumbers are not permitted to operate it.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service lines

That means if your job needs the water shut off at the street — a service line replacement, a meter issue, a failed main shut-off inside the house that won't hold — the shut-off gets arranged through The City rather than done by whoever's in your driveway. Build that into your timeline. A crew that shows up expecting to work and then has to wait on a valve turn has just cost you a day.

It also means your inside shut-off is the one that saves you in an emergency. Find it before you need it. In most Calgary homes it's in the basement near the water heater on the street-facing wall, and knowing where that handle is turns a flooded basement into a wet floor. What to do in the first hour when sewage appears in your basement

This is the sequence that limits the damage and protects your position afterward.

  1. Stop using water immediately. No flushing, no laundry, no dishwasher, no showers — and tell everyone in the house. If the line out is blocked, every litre you send down is looking for the lowest opening in your basement, which is the floor drain you're standing next to.
  2. Get people and belongings out of it. Sewage is a health hazard, not just a mess. Boxes off the floor, kids and pets out of the room, and don't wade through it in bare feet or run a shop vac you care about.
  3. Photograph everything before you clean. Wide shots of the room, close shots of the water line on the wall, the drain itself, and every damaged item. This is what your insurance claim is built on, and you only get one chance to take these.
  4. Call 311. Ahead of calling a plumber. The agent works through whether this looks like internal plumbing or possibly City infrastructure, and dispatches accordingly. If several homes on your block are backing up at once, say so — that pattern points at the main, not at you.
  5. Call your insurer. Ask specifically whether you have sewer backup coverage and what they need from you. Most policies have notification expectations and you don't want to discover yours late.
  6. Then call a plumber. If 311 determines the blockage is on private property, that's your call to make, and it should include a camera pass — not just an auger. Clearing the blockage without finding out why it happened means you're doing this again. What a proper drain cleaning involves covers the difference.

What City crews will and won't do

Worth knowing before they arrive so you're not expecting the wrong thing. The City states plainly that crews will not move furniture, pull back carpet, remove toilets or make holes in drywall to identify the source of a backup. Their job is to determine whether City infrastructure is involved. If the blockage is on private property, you're advised to call a plumber.

Source: The City of Calgary — Sewage backup

Two things we deliberately won't tell you, because we can't verify them and other sites state them as fact: whether The City charges anything when 311 dispatches a crew and the problem turns out to be private-side, and whether there's a formal compensation or damage-claim process when a City-side failure floods your basement. Ask 311 both questions directly while you have them on the phone — they're exactly the sort of thing worth having a clear answer on, and we'd rather send you to the source than guess on your behalf.

The water line has its own rule: 15 days

The sewer gets all the attention, but the water service line is the one with a deadline attached. Under the Water Utility Bylaw, an owner must repair a confirmed service line leak within 15 days, or The City may issue a remedial order. That's a genuinely short window once you factor in getting quotes, booking a crew and scheduling a shut-off at the street.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service line leaks

A leaking service line is also silently expensive while you decide what to do about it. Water you never used still runs through your meter, and Calgary bills water and wastewater volume together. If your bill has jumped and nobody can find the cause inside the house, the buried line is a real candidate — how to check your meter for a silent leak walks through the test.

Three ways it gets repaired

The reason to ask which method a contractor is quoting is that the cheapest line item isn't always the cheapest outcome. A spot repair on a pipe with three more weak joints in it is a down payment on the next dig, and a trenchless pull that saves a 40-year-old spruce has value that doesn't show up on the invoice.

What you can do before anything goes wrong

  • Locate your Real Property Report now. Ten minutes in a filing cabinet today saves an argument on the worst day.
  • Know your line's likely material. Pre-1970 inner-city Calgary generally means clay tile sewer and possibly galvanized water supply. Both have predictable failure modes, which makes budgeting a plan instead of a shock.
  • Camera the sewer once if the house is old and you've never seen it. A single inspection tells you whether replacement is a five-year question or a twenty-year one.
  • Fit backflow protection if your basement is finished. Owning the pipe doesn't mean you can stop a surcharged main coming back at you — a backwater valve does, and a plumbing permit is required to install one.
  • Ask your broker about a service line endorsement. Base policies commonly don't cover the buried pipe itself; that's the add-on that addresses roots, corrosion and shifting earth.
