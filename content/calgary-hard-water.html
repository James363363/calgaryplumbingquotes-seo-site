Short answer: Calgary's water is hard to very hard, and there is no single number for the city because two treatment plants feed it. In 2025 the Bearspaw plant ran roughly 141–200 mg/L of calcium carbonate and the Glenmore plant ran roughly 181–274 mg/L. Divided by 17.1, that's about 8–12 grains per gallon on the Bearspaw side and 10.6–16.0 grains on the Glenmore side. Both are hard enough to cost you appliance life.

Most pages about Calgary hard water quote one grains-per-gallon figure for the whole city. That can't be right for everyone, because the two plants are measurably different and both of them move through the year. How we converted it

One grain per gallon equals 17.1 mg/L of calcium carbonate, so grains per gallon = mg/L ÷ 17.1. Worked example: Glenmore's winter high of 274 mg/L ÷ 17.1 = 16.0 gpg. Bearspaw's spring low of 141 ÷ 17.1 = 8.2 gpg. Figures above are rounded to one decimal. If you're sizing a softener, use the mg/L numbers and let the installer do their own conversion rather than inheriting ours.

Where that lands on the standard scale

The classification most water professionals use comes from the US Geological Survey and is stated in mg/L of calcium carbonate: soft is 0–60, moderately hard is 61–120, hard is 121–180, and anything above 180 is very hard.

Source: USGS — Hardness of water.

Put Calgary against that and the picture is blunt. Bearspaw sits in the hard band and crosses into very hard at its seasonal peaks. Glenmore is above 180 mg/L in every single quarter of 2025 — very hard, all year, with the winter numbers roughly half again as hard as the softest Bearspaw water. That's not a marketing framing; it's the City's own data against a neutral scale.

We can't tell you which plant serves your house

The City doesn't publish a neighbourhood map of plant coverage, and the distribution system can shift load between them. Anyone who tells you your community is definitely on Bearspaw or definitely on Glenmore is guessing. If you want to know — and it's genuinely worth knowing before you size a softener — call 311 and ask. That's the only reliable route we know of.

What hard water actually does in a Calgary house

Hardness isn't a contaminant. The City is clear that there are no known health effects from the calcium and magnesium in drinking water. What it is, is a slow tax on everything that heats or restricts water.

It shortens hot water tank life

Heat the water and the minerals precipitate out as solid scale, settling on the tank floor right where a gas burner fires. That layer insulates the steel from the water, so the burner runs longer and the metal runs hotter than designed — which is what cracks the glass lining. On an electric tank the scale plates straight onto the elements. It's a major reason Calgary tanks come in under the lifespan the box implies. Full detail here.

It clogs aerators and cartridges

The screen at the end of your kitchen tap silts up with white crust, and so does the mixing cartridge behind the handle. That's why one fixture goes weak while the rest of the house is fine — and why unscrewing an aerator and soaking it in vinegar is the highest-value ten minutes in home plumbing. If everything has gone weak at once, it's a different problem.

It wastes soap and leaves scum

Calcium and magnesium react with soap instead of lathering with it, so you use more detergent to get the same result and you get the grey film on tub walls and shower doors. That's a real running cost and a real cleaning chore, not an aesthetic complaint — it's the thing most people actually buy a softener to solve.

It spots the dishwasher load

Those white specks on glassware are mineral deposits left when hard water evaporates. Rinse aid masks it; it doesn't remove the cause. The same deposits build up on the machine's heating element and jets, which is how a dishwasher quietly gets worse at its job before it fails.

It ages appliances that heat water

Kettles, coffee machines, humidifiers, washing machines, dishwashers — anything with a heating element in contact with our water accumulates scale on that element. Descaling regularly buys you years, and in Calgary it should be on your list rather than something you get around to.

It doesn't cause low pressure on its own

Worth saying because it gets blamed for everything. Scale narrows fixture openings and old galvanized pipe, but modern copper and PEX don't scale shut in the way people imagine. If your whole house has gone weak, look at the pressure reducing valve, a partly-closed main, or corroded galvanized supply first.

Do you need a softener? The honest version

Nobody needs one on health grounds, and it's notable that The City takes no position in favour of them. Its guidance is that consumers should "thoroughly research the various water softener systems available before deciding," it notes salt-based systems "may not be suitable for people on sodium-reduced diets," and it states there are "no known health effects associated with calcium and magnesium minerals in drinking water." That is a public utility deliberately declining to endorse a product category, and it's a more useful signal than any softener company's page.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water quality and water hardness.

So the question isn't need, it's whether it's worth it to you. Here's how we'd frame it:

  • Buy one mainly for the soap, laundry and cleaning. That's the benefit softening reliably delivers — lather, no scum ring, less detergent, softer laundry. If those are your daily irritations, the money does what you want.
  • Don't buy one purely to save your hot water tank. It helps, but an annual flush plus an anode rod check around year five protects the tank for a tiny fraction of the cost. Do the cheap thing first and see whether the expensive thing is still worth it.
  • Take the sodium caveat seriously if it applies to you. Ion exchange works by trading calcium and magnesium for sodium, so softened water carries more sodium than what came in. Anyone in the house on a sodium-restricted diet should know that. The standard fix is a hard-water tap at the kitchen sink for drinking and cooking — ask for it to be quoted in, not added later.
  • Factor the ongoing cost, not just the install. A salt-based unit needs salt bought and carried down the basement stairs, and it discharges brine on each regeneration. Metered-regeneration units use less salt and less water than old timer-based ones, which matters given water and wastewater are both billed by volume in Calgary.
  • Watch the plumbing detail. Softened water usually feeds the hot side and interior cold, leaving outdoor taps and often the kitchen cold on hard water. Where the bypass goes is worth deciding before install day.

The salt-free alternatives, assessed honestly

Salt-free "conditioners" — usually template-assisted crystallisation, sometimes sold as scale inhibitors or descalers — work on a different principle. They don't remove anything. They give dissolved minerals a surface to form microscopic crystals on, so the hardness stays suspended in the water rather than plating onto pipe walls and heating elements.

What that means in practice: the water is still just as hard. You will still get spots on glassware, you will still get soap scum, and your laundry will not feel different. What you may get is less scale accumulating on heated surfaces. If you buy one expecting soft water, you'll be disappointed, and some of the marketing does not make this distinction clearly enough.

On the evidence: we'd rather flag this than gloss it. There's lab work supporting scale reduction from template-assisted crystallisation, but the independent, long-run field evidence is thinner than for ion exchange, which has decades of it. Treat salt-free as reasonable when brine or sodium is the deciding factor, and as a weaker bet when what you want is soft water.

Two other things sold in this space: whole-house filters, which handle taste, chlorine and sediment but do nothing for hardness; and magnetic or electronic descalers, where the independent evidence is weak enough that we wouldn't spend money on one.

What it costs — planning ranges only

There is no authoritative public source for water treatment pricing in Calgary, so treat everything here as a planning range for budgeting, not a quote. If you're not buying anything, do these instead

  1. Flush the hot water tank once a year. That's the single biggest thing you can do about hardness for free. One honest exception: if the tank is well past ten and has never been flushed, disturbing the sediment can start a leak — read this first.
  2. Soak your aerators in vinegar twice a year. Unscrew, soak overnight, scrub, refit. Restores flow at the tap and takes ten minutes.
  3. Descale the kettle and coffee machine on a schedule. Not when it looks bad — on a schedule. Elements that stay clean last dramatically longer.
  4. Run the dishwasher's own descale cycle, or a citric acid cycle. Rinse aid handles the spots you can see; this handles the deposits on the element you can't.
  5. Wipe the shower glass down. Boring, but scum removed wet is a cloth and scum left to dry is an afternoon with a scraper.

None of that is glamorous and none of it is billable, which is why you don't read it on many plumbing sites. It's also most of the benefit for none of the money.
