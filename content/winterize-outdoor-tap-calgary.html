Short answer: Yes, you need to drain it, and there are five steps — not two. Close the interior shut-off valve for the outdoor line, go outside and open the outdoor tap fully, come back in and open the small bleeder cap on that interior valve to let the trapped water out, leave the outdoor tap open all winter, and take the hose off. Doing the first two steps and skipping the bleeder is the mistake that bursts pipes in this city, because the water left sitting between the interior valve and the spout has nowhere to escape when it expands.

Ten minutes, no parts, no skill required, and it prevents one of the most common and most expensive winter failures in Calgary homes. Best done in early October, not late November — more on that below.

The sequence, in order, with the reason for each step

Every step exists because of the one before it. Out of order, and you leave water where it can freeze.

  1. Find the interior shut-off for the outdoor line. A small valve on the pipe feeding the hose bib, inside the wall the tap comes through — usually the basement joist bay directly behind the tap. Gate valve with a round handle or ball valve with a lever, and there's normally a small brass cap or thumbscrew on its side. That's the bleeder. Two outdoor taps means two valves. Some houses have none, which changes things — see below.
  2. Close that valve. Clockwise on a round handle until it stops firmly, or a quarter turn on a lever so it sits crosswise to the pipe. That isolates the outdoor section from your heated household plumbing, so only the short outdoor run is exposed.
  3. Go outside and open the outdoor tap fully. Take the hose off first. Opening the tap gives the trapped water an exit at the far end and lets air in behind it. Some water will run out — that's the point. Without this, step four has nothing to drain.
  4. Go back inside and open the bleeder. Cup or rag underneath, unscrew the little cap or thumbscrew on the side of the interior valve, let the water drain. This is the step almost everyone skips. That water is sitting in the section between the closed interior valve and the outdoor spout — the exact stretch running through the cold rim joist and exterior wall. Water expands when it freezes, and in a sealed section it has nowhere to go but through the pipe wall. Let it dribble until it stops, then leave the cap loose for the winter so any residual drop can escape.
  5. Leave the outdoor tap open until spring. An open spout means water that finds its way in can drain out and any ice can expand outward rather than against a closed valve. Closing it in December because it looks untidy undoes the whole job. In April, close the outdoor tap first, snug the bleeder cap, then open the interior valve slowly and look at the pipe.

Don't force a stuck valve. An interior shut-off that hasn't moved in fifteen years can seize, or the packing starts weeping when you turn it — old gate valves in Calgary basements do this routinely. Snug it as far as it goes comfortably, don't lean on it with a wrench, and if it won't close or starts dripping at the stem, that's a small planned repair now instead of an emergency later.

Frost-free sillcock or standard hose bib? How to tell

Two very different fittings, and knowing which one you have changes what winterizing means.

Standard hose bibFrost-free (frost-proof) sillcock
How it worksThe valve seat is right behind the handle, at the outside wallA long stem runs back through the wall so the valve seat closes 15–30 cm inside the heated space
What you see outsideShort body, sits close against the sidingSame short body outside — you cannot tell by looking at the outside
How to actually tellInterior pipe joins the tap right at the wallInterior pipe joins a long tube that penetrates the wall; from inside you'll see 15–30 cm of tube before the connection
Handle feelShuts off instantly, right at your handSlight delay between turning and the flow stopping — the seat is metres of stem away
Does it still need draining?Absolutely. It holds water in the exterior wall by designIt's designed to self-drain — but see the hose warning below

Older Calgary homes — pre-1960 inner-city stock and plenty of 1970s and 1980s suburban builds — typically have standard bibs with an interior shut-off and bleeder. Newer builds, and anything where the tap has been replaced, usually have a frost-free sillcock. Plenty of houses have one of each.

A frost-free sillcock still bursts if you leave the hose on

The most important thing on this page for anyone with a newer tap. A frost-free unit works by draining itself: shut it off and the water in the long stem runs out through the spout by gravity. Leave a hose attached and the hose blocks that path — the stem can't empty, water stays in the tube inside your wall, it freezes, and the copper splits. The unit did nothing wrong; it just couldn't drain.

So the rule is the same for both types: the hose comes off in the fall, every year, no exceptions. The City's own frozen pipe guidance lists disconnecting garden hoses alongside its other winter measures, right next to keeping the home at a minimum of 15 °C and using only UL-listed heat tape.

Source: The City of Calgary — Frozen water lines

The other frost-free failure: it was never installed on a slope

For a frost-free sillcock to drain, it has to be installed with a slight downward pitch to the outside — a couple of degrees is enough. Fitted dead level, or pitched slightly back into the house because that's how the hole through the rim joist lined up, it holds a pool of water in the stem permanently. It works perfectly for years, then splits in a hard cold snap, and the homeowner concludes frost-free products are a scam. They aren't; that one was installed wrong. If you can see the interior stem, sight along it — level or worse means treat it like a standard bib and bleed it every fall.

Why you find out in April, not January

The Calgary pattern is remarkably consistent. The pipe splits in January inside the exterior wall. Nothing happens, because the interior valve is closed and the split section is dry and full of ice, so you forget about it. In May you go out to wash the car, open the interior valve, turn the tap on — and water pours into the basement, the wall cavity, or the finished ceiling under the deck.

Which is why the first spring use should be deliberate: open the interior valve slowly with someone standing at the outdoor tap, then look at the interior pipe with a flashlight while the water runs. A spray, a drip at a joint or a suspicious bulge in the copper means shut it straight back off. Do that test when you have half an hour, not when the kids are waiting with the sprinkler.

If it does let go, the response is the same as any other burst: main shut-off, power off to the wet area, photograph everything before you clean up. We've written that out step by step on our frozen and burst pipe page.

Do it in October, not November

People wait for winter to feel like it's arriving, which in Calgary is a bad bet. We routinely get a hard overnight freeze in the first half of October while the days are still pleasant, and that first freeze is enough — the exterior wall cavity gets colder than the air outside and the pipe holds no heat.

Tie it to something you already do: the day you drain the hose and hang it in the garage is the day. Hose off, interior valve closed, tap open, bleeder open. Irrigation system blown out the same week. And if the tap is on a garage wall, the pipe feeding it runs through unheated space for its whole length — garage walls are one of the most reliable freeze locations in this city, along with crawl spaces and rim joists.

  • Hose off, every tap, every fall. The single highest-return five seconds of home maintenance in Calgary.
  • Both taps. Front and back. It's always the one round the side that gets forgotten.
  • Splitter and timer off too. A brass Y-splitter left on the spout holds water and cracks, and it blocks a frost-free unit from draining.
  • Check the garage tap and the laundry tub line. Same cold, less attention.
  • Note where the interior valve is. A bit of tape on the joist. Next October's version of you will be grateful.

What if there's no interior shut-off at all?

Some houses genuinely don't — a tap teed straight off a household line with no isolating valve and no bleeder. If that's you and it's a standard hose bib, there's no safe way to winterize it, because you can't isolate or drain the outdoor section. Adding an interior shut-off with a bleeder, or swapping the bib for a properly pitched frost-free sillcock, turns that tap from an annual gamble into a non-issue. Either is a short job, and one worth doing in September rather than as an emergency in February.

It doesn't need a permit, either. The City requires one when a plumbing system is constructed, extended or altered, but not to repair a leak in a water distribution system or replace existing faucets and fixtures — a like-for-like hose bib swap sits on the no-permit side. Add a new outdoor tap where none existed and that's an extension, so the answer changes. Our permits and DIY rules page has the full list.
