Short answer: Yes, you need to drain it, and there are five steps — not two. Close the interior shut-off valve for the outdoor line, go outside and open the outdoor tap fully, come back in and open the small bleeder cap on that interior valve to let the trapped water out, leave the outdoor tap open all winter, and take the hose off. Skipping the bleeder is the mistake that bursts pipes in this city: the water left between the interior valve and the spout has nowhere to escape when it freezes and expands.

Ten minutes, no parts, no skill required, and it prevents one of the most common and most expensive winter failures in Calgary homes. Best done in early October, not late November — more on that below.

The sequence, in order, with the reason for each step

Every step exists because of the one before it. Out of order, and you leave water where it can freeze.

  1. Find the interior shut-off for the outdoor line. A small valve on the pipe feeding the hose bib, usually in the basement joist bay directly behind the tap. Round-handled gate valve or lever-handled ball valve, with a small brass cap or thumbscrew on its side — that cap is the bleeder. Two outdoor taps means two valves. Some houses have none, which changes things — see below.
  2. Close that valve. Clockwise on a round handle until it stops firmly, or a quarter turn on a lever so it sits crosswise to the pipe. That isolates the outdoor section from your heated household plumbing, so only the short outdoor run is exposed.
  3. Go outside and open the outdoor tap fully. Take the hose off first. That gives the trapped water an exit at the far end and lets air in behind it. Some water will run out — that's the point. Without this, step four has nothing to drain.
  4. Go back inside and open the bleeder. Cup or rag underneath, unscrew the little cap or thumbscrew on the side of the interior valve, let the water drain. This is the step almost everyone skips, and the water it releases is sitting in the worst possible place — the stretch running through the cold rim joist and exterior wall. Let it dribble until it stops, then leave the cap loose for the winter so any residual drop can escape.
  5. Leave the outdoor tap open until spring. An open spout means water that finds its way in can drain out and any ice can expand outward rather than against a closed valve. Closing it in December because it looks untidy undoes the whole job. Reopening in spring has its own short sequence, further down this page.

Don't force a stuck valve. An interior shut-off that hasn't moved in fifteen years can seize, or start weeping at the packing when you turn it — old gate valves in Calgary basements do this routinely, and scale off our famously hard water is part of why. Snug it as far as it goes comfortably, don't lean on it with a wrench, and if it won't close or drips at the stem, that's a small planned repair now instead of an emergency later.

Frost-free sillcock or standard hose bib? How to tell

Two very different fittings, and knowing which one you have changes what winterizing means.

Standard hose bibFrost-free (frost-proof) sillcock
How it worksThe valve seat is right behind the handle, at the outside wallA long stem runs back through the wall so the valve seat closes 15–30 cm inside the heated space
What you see outsideShort body against the sidingIdentical from outside — you cannot tell by looking
How to actually tellInterior pipe joins the tap right at the wallFrom inside, 15–30 cm of tube runs through the wall before the connection
Handle feelShuts off instantly, right at your handSlight delay between turning and the flow stopping — the seat is the length of the stem away
Does it still need draining?Absolutely. It holds water in the exterior wall by designIt's designed to self-drain — but see the hose warning below

Older Calgary homes — pre-1960 inner-city stock and plenty of 1970s and 1980s suburban builds — typically have standard bibs with an interior shut-off and bleeder. Newer builds, and any tap that's been replaced, usually have a frost-free sillcock. Plenty of houses have one of each.

A frost-free sillcock still bursts if you leave the hose on

The most important line on this page for anyone with a newer tap. A frost-free unit drains itself: shut it off and the water in the long stem runs out through the spout by gravity. A hose left attached blocks that path, so the stem can't empty, the water stays in the tube inside your wall, and the copper splits. The unit did nothing wrong; it just couldn't drain.

So the rule is the same for both types: the hose comes off in the fall, every year, no exceptions. The City's own frozen pipe guidance puts it first: make sure the water line to outside faucets and garden hoses is turned off, disconnected and drained. It sits alongside the City's other winter measures: a thermostat set to at least 15 °C, and UL-listed heat tape or cable if you insulate exposed pipe.

Source: The City of Calgary — Protect your water lines and meter from freezing (checked August 2026)

The other frost-free failure: it was never installed on a slope

For a frost-free sillcock to drain, it has to sit with a slight downward pitch to the outside — a couple of degrees is enough. Fitted dead level, or tipped back into the house because that's how the hole through the rim joist lined up, it holds a permanent pool of water in the stem. It works fine for years, then splits in a hard cold snap, and the homeowner concludes frost-free products are a scam. They aren't; that one was installed wrong. If you can see the interior stem, sight along it — level or worse means treat it like a standard bib and bleed it every fall.

Why you find out in April, not January

The Calgary pattern is consistent. The pipe splits in January inside the exterior wall, and nothing happens — the interior valve is closed, the split section is dry and full of ice, so you forget about it. In May you go to wash the car, open the interior valve, turn the tap on, and water pours into the basement or the wall cavity.

Which is why the first spring use should be deliberate. Close the outdoor tap, snug the bleeder cap, then open the interior valve slowly with someone standing outside, and watch the interior pipe with a flashlight while the water runs. A spray, a drip at a joint or a suspicious bulge in the copper means shut it straight back off. Do that when you have half an hour, not when the kids are waiting with the sprinkler.

If it does let go, the response is the same as any other burst: main shut-off, power off to the wet area, photograph everything before you clean up. We've written that out step by step on our frozen and burst pipe page, and the rest of the winter list lives on the main Calgary plumbing guide.

Do it in October, not November

People wait for winter to feel like it's arriving, which in Calgary is a bad bet. A hard overnight freeze in the first half of October, while the days are still pleasant, is enough on its own — the exterior wall cavity gets colder than the air outside and the pipe holds no heat.

So tie it to something you already do: the day you drain the hose and hang it in the garage is the day. Hose off, interior valve closed, tap open, bleeder open, irrigation blown out the same week. If the tap is on a garage wall, the pipe feeding it runs through unheated space for its whole length, which makes garage walls one of the most reliable freeze locations in this city. The same cold snap starts the other failure homeowners get caught by — a frozen sewer line, which shows up as slow drains rather than a burst.

  • Both taps, hoses off. Front and back. It's always the one round the side that gets forgotten.
  • Splitter and timer off too. A brass Y-splitter holds water, cracks, and blocks a frost-free unit from draining.
  • Check the garage tap. Same cold, less attention.
  • Label the interior valve. A bit of tape on the joist. Next October's version of you will be grateful.

What if there's no interior shut-off at all?

Some houses genuinely don't — a tap teed straight off a household line, no isolating valve, no bleeder. If that's you and it's a standard hose bib, there's no safe way to winterize it: you can't isolate or drain the outdoor section. Adding an interior shut-off with a bleeder, or swapping the bib for a properly pitched frost-free sillcock, turns that tap from an annual gamble into a non-issue — both short jobs in September rather than emergencies in February.

It doesn't need a permit, either. As of August 2026 the City requires one when a plumbing system is constructed, extended or altered, but not to repair a leak in a water distribution or drainage system, and not to replace existing faucets and fixtures — a like-for-like hose bib swap sits on the no-permit side. Add a new outdoor tap where none existed and that's an extension, so the answer changes. Our permits and DIY rules page has the full list, including what a homeowner's permit costs and why that number moves every January.
