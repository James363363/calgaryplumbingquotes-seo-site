Cold shower, and the tank in the basement is either silent or making a noise it didn't used to make. Two things decide what happens next: whether anything is leaking, and whether you smell gas. Deal with those first, then work down the checklist — roughly a third of the no-hot-water calls we take turn out to be something the homeowner could have sorted in five minutes, and we'd genuinely rather you knew which one you've got before you pay anyone.

If you smell gas, stop reading and get out

Don't look for the source. Don't relight the pilot. Don't flip switches, unplug anything or use your phone inside the house. Get everyone and any pets outside, leave the door open behind you, and from outside call ATCO Gas's 24-hour emergency line (it's on your gas bill) or 911. A plumber is the wrong first call. Nobody will think less of you for a false alarm.

Is it leaking? Do this first

A tank that isn't heating is an inconvenience. A tank that's leaking is a clock. Check the floor around the base before anything else.

If there's a small amount of water and you're not sure it's a leak: dry the floor, lay down paper towel, and check again in two hours. Cold Calgary supply water hitting a tank in a humid basement produces real condensation, and so does a big hot-water draw — plenty of "leaks" dry up and never come back. If the paper is wet again, treat it as a leak.

The shut-off sequence

  1. Shut the water off at the interior main. In most Calgary homes it's in the basement on the wall facing the street, frequently in sight of the water heater itself — a round handle or a quarter-turn lever, sometimes with the meter beside it. If your tank has its own cold-water shut-off on the inlet pipe at the top, closing that isolates the tank alone and leaves the rest of the house with water, which is the better outcome if it works.
  2. Turn the gas control to OFF, or kill the breaker. On a gas tank there's a dial or switch on the control valve near the floor — turn it to OFF, not to PILOT. On an electric tank, switch off the dedicated breaker at the panel. This matters because a tank that keeps heating while it empties can damage the elements or the burner assembly and turn a replacement into a bigger one.
  3. Get the water moving away from anything expensive. Towels, a wet-vac if you have one, and lift anything off the floor nearby. Water damage costs are cumulative and the restoration bill routinely exceeds the plumbing bill.
  4. Then call. With the water and fuel off, you've stopped the damage and bought yourself the ability to make a decision rather than a panic purchase.

Do not touch the curb stop outside

The valve under the little metal cap near your sidewalk is the water service valve. It sits right about at the property line, and The City of Calgary owns it — service valves outside the property line are City equipment. The valve you are responsible for is the main shut-off inside the house, so use that one. People pop the cap with a screwdriver, damage the valve or the operating rod, and add a City repair to the bill they already had.

The five-minute checklist before you call anyone

Nothing here involves tools, gas fittings or opening anything sealed. Run through it; if one of these is your answer, you've just saved yourself a service call, and that's the whole point of this page.

  • Work out whether you're on gas or electric. A metal flue pipe going up from the top of the tank, and a small access panel near the floor, means gas. No flue and a heavy cable running into the side means electric. Everything after this depends on which one you have.
  • Electric: check the breaker. A tripped water-heater breaker can look "on" — push it firmly to OFF, then back to ON. Also check for a small red reset button behind the upper access panel cover on the tank; that's the high-limit switch, and it trips. If either one trips again straight away, stop and call. Something is drawing more than it should.
  • Gas: is the pilot lit? Look through the little window at the base or pop the outer access cover. No flame means no heat, and the relighting instructions are printed on a label on the tank. Follow that label, not a video for a different model.
  • Gas: check the control dial isn't on PILOT or VACATION. It happens more than you'd think, usually after somebody's been away, or after a furnace service where things got switched around. Turn it to ON and give it 30–45 minutes.
  • Check the gas supply valve on the pipe feeding the tank. Handle in line with the pipe means open; across the pipe means closed. Don't force anything — if it doesn't move easily, leave it.
  • Check the thermostat setting. Somebody may have turned it down. Most tanks run around 49–60 °C; lower saves gas but also means you run out faster, which reads to everyone in the house as "no hot water."
  • Ask whether it's really no hot water, or not enough. Scalding-then-cold in three minutes is a different fault from stone cold at the tap. Not enough usually means a broken dip tube, a failed lower element, or a decade of sediment eating your tank volume — and in Calgary, sediment is the usual suspect.

If you're mid-cold-snap and you also have no heat, check the furnace before you assume the tank. A furnace and a water heater on the same gas line can go out together for the same reason, and heat is the more urgent of the two. Related: frozen and burst pipes.

What's actually broken: narrowing it down

Knowing the likely culprit before you call is worth real money — it's the difference between accepting a quote and understanding it.

One Calgary-specific note running through all of this: our water is hard — the City's 2025 figures put Bearspaw around 141–200 mg/L and Glenmore around 181–274 mg/L of calcium carbonate, which converts at 17.1 mg/L per grain to roughly 8–12 and 11–16 grains per gallon respectively. That scale coats burner surfaces and elements, which makes them run hotter and fail sooner than the same part would in a soft-water city. It's why "the part failed early" is a normal outcome here rather than a sign you were sold a lemon.

Tonight or tomorrow? The honest version

We'd rather tell you to wait and earn the call in the morning than take a night booking you didn't need.

Call now

Water is actively spreading, or the tank is above a finished basement or on carpet. Damage compounds by the hour and the restoration bill is usually the bigger one — paying a premium to stop it at midnight is straightforwardly cheaper than replacing a ceiling.

Call now

It's deep winter, the house is also losing heat, or the property is about to sit empty. In a Calgary cold snap a stalled heating appliance can become a frozen-pipe problem overnight, which is a much larger bill than the one in front of you.

Wait until morning

Nothing is leaking and you just have cold water. Boil a kettle, shower somewhere else, book at day rates. You'll pay less and you'll get a proper diagnostic instead of a fast fix — and you'll have time to get a second quote if it turns out to be a replacement.

The cost gap is real and worth naming. Plumbing companies around Calgary publish independent rates of roughly $85–$120 an hour, company rates of $95–$150, and emergency or after-hours rates of $150–$250. Those are self-reported figures from a competitor's own page, not an authoritative source — and there isn't one. No public body publishes plumber rates for this city, so treat every number you see online, including these, as a planning estimate rather than a quote.

If it turns out to be a replacement

Ask for the quote itemised: the tank, the labour, the permit if one is needed, and hauling the old unit away. That's how you tell whether two quotes are actually comparable. Our page on replacing a hot water tank in Calgary covers what each of those lines should look like and where the published prices come from.

On permits — as of August 2026, The City requires a plumbing permit when a plumbing system is constructed, extended or altered, so a straight like-for-like swap generally isn't caught but changing size, location or venting generally is. Gas is stricter: a homeowner can't pull a gas permit in Calgary unless they hold Alberta first- or second-class gasfitter certification and reside at the address, so on a gas tank a licensed contractor pulls it.
