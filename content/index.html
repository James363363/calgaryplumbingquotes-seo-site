Plumbing in Calgary is its own thing

Generic advice from a plumbing blog based in Toronto or Texas will get a lot of this wrong. Four things make this city different, and they shape most of what actually goes wrong here.

The cold is not theoretical

Calgary swings hard. A chinook can put you above freezing in February and a week later you're at −30 °C. That freeze–thaw cycling is harder on plumbing than steady cold, because it's the repeated expansion that finds the weak joint. The pipes that fail here are the ones in unheated or barely-heated space: garage walls, crawl spaces, rim joists, and the run out to an outdoor tap somebody forgot to drain in October. The City advises keeping your home at a minimum of 15 °C to protect your service line — which matters most for the houses nobody's living in over the winter.

Our water is hard, and it shortens equipment life

This is measurable, not marketing. The City publishes hardness figures for both treatment plants: Bearspaw runs roughly 141–200 mg/L of calcium carbonate through the year, and Glenmore runs roughly 181–274 mg/L. The City doesn't publish grains per gallon, so we converted it ourselves — divide the mg/L figure by 17.1 and you're looking at about 8–12 grains per gallon on the Bearspaw side and 10.6–16.0 on the Glenmore side — "hard" to "very hard." That scale settles in the bottom of your hot water tank, coats element and burner surfaces, and clogs aerators and cartridges. It's a major reason Calgary tanks don't hit the lifespan printed on the box.

Half the pipe in this city is older than the people living above it

Calgary grew in distinct waves and each one left its own plumbing signature. Pre-1960 inner-city: galvanized supply, clay tile sewer, cast iron stacks. The 1975–1997 suburban boom: Poly-B supply lines. Post-2000: PEX and ABS, generally fine, but with backwater valve requirements that older homes were never held to. Knowing your home's era narrows the diagnosis before anyone opens a wall.

The property line decides who pays

This is the one that costs people the most money to learn late. On both water and sewer, The City owns the pipe from the main to your property line, and you own it from the property line to your house — and you pay for all repairs on that private-side portion, with the water meter as the one exception. That property line is usually only a few metres in from the curb. A failure two metres on the wrong side of it is entirely your bill.
