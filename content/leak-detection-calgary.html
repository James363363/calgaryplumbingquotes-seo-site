Short answer: Finding a hidden leak is a diagnostic job, not a repair, and the right first call depends on where the water is. If the leak might be on the buried water service between the curb and your house, phone 311 before you phone a plumber — The City of Calgary dispatches leak locators to that, and it costs you nothing. If the water is inside the house, that's a paid diagnostic, and no Calgary company publishes a price for it.

You've got evidence and no source. A bill that jumped, a damp patch on a finished wall, a meter that won't sit still, a strip of lawn that greens up first every spring. Every leak-detection page in this city says the same thing — advanced equipment, fast response, book now — and none tell you the thing that decides what this costs: whose pipe the water is coming out of.

Three places a leak hides, and who you call for each

Sort your symptoms before you dial. It decides whether this is a free City visit or a paid one.

Inside the house

A fixture, a supply line in a wall, a drain under a floor. Symptoms are local — a stain, a soft spot, a musty smell in one room. This is the paid diagnostic, and where equipment earns its keep: the alternative is opening drywall on a guess.

Out in the yard, on your water service

The buried line from the property line to the house. It often shows as nothing indoors — the water never passes your meter, so it may never reach your bill. Look for ground that stays unfrozen in winter, grass that greens early, or water you can hear at the main shut-off. Start with 311.

On the drainage side

A cracked or root-invaded sewer line, a failed joint under the floor. No pressure behind it, so it shows as smell, repeat blockages or sinking ground, not a spreading wet patch. Camera job, not a listening job. Why a drain that keeps blocking is a symptom →

If it might be the buried service line, phone 311 first

Almost no plumbing page in Calgary tells you this, and it can save the whole cost of a private investigation. The City employs leak locators, dispatched through 311 when a citizen suspects a leak. When they aren't at emergency main breaks, those crews run scheduled leak detection surveys.

Sources: The City of Calgary — Water service leaks on private property (PDF); Water service line leaks in Calgary. Confirmed August 2026.

The test that sorts your side from The City's is simple enough to follow. In The City's words, a leak on private property "can be determined by turning off the water at the shut-off valve. If the leak stops, repairing the leak is the homeowner's responsibility." A locator will contact you for access to the meter or main shut-off inside. So the free visit doesn't just confirm a leak — it tells you who owns it, which is the answer with a dollar figure attached. Where the property line sits, and why it decides the bill →

Know what the clock does. Once a leak on your side is confirmed, The City requires the repair within 15 days or a remedial order may be issued — a short runway once you've collected quotes. The City's guidance also warns these repairs "aren't typically covered by a basic homeowner's insurance policy," so check your wording before you assume.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service leaks on private property (PDF)

The City lists three repair routes, and they are not the same job in your front yard. A service pull is trenchless, pulling a replacement service from the house to the valve with minimal digging. Excavating is a trench from the valve to the house. A spot repair uses hydrovac to expose the pipe at one defect. Ask which is being quoted: price and lawn damage differ enormously, and The City publishes no cost figures. Waiting isn't free either — The City notes an unrepaired leak can saturate soil, seep into your basement or a neighbour's, and damage your foundation.

Two free tests, before you pay anyone

  1. Run the meter test. Turn off every tap and water-using appliance — dishwasher, washer, ice maker, humidifier, irrigation timer — then watch the flow register. Still moving means a leak inside the house. Dead still, with symptoms you can't explain, points outside at the buried service.
  2. Do the toilet dye test. Food colouring in the tank, wait ten minutes, look in the bowl without flushing. Colour means the flapper is passing water. The City lists toilets first among common leak locations, ahead of irrigation, humidifier valves, taps and hose bibs.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water leaks and basic repairs. Confirmed August 2026.

What the equipment does — and what it can't

Four tools do nearly all of this work, and knowing which your symptom calls for stops you paying for a truck roll that was never going to find it.

Acoustic listening

Amplifies the sound of pressurised water escaping a pipe, narrowing a leak behind a wall or under a slab to a small area. The benefit is subtraction — you open one square of drywall instead of chasing a stain across a room. It needs pressure behind the water, so: supply lines, not drains.

Thermal imaging

Reads surface temperature, so wet material inside a wall shows as a cool shape long before it shows as a stain. That maps how far the water travelled, so you know how much has to come out — but it sees the footprint, not the pipe itself.

Pressure and isolation testing

Closing off sections and watching whether pressure holds tells you which branch is losing water. Unglamorous, and often the step that saves the most money: ruling out three-quarters of the house makes the other tools cheap to point.

Camera inspection

A camera goes down drains and sewers, not through supply piping — so it answers cracked-pipe and root questions, not the damp-wall question. Right call when the symptom is repeat blockages or sewer smell. What a sewer camera actually shows →

What makes a Calgary leak a Calgary leak

Most leak-detection content online was written for a housing stock that isn't ours.

Slab leaks are largely an imported idea

American content is dominated by slab leaks because much of the United States builds slab-on-grade, with supply piping cast into the floor. Calgary builds basements. The local equivalents are different: a supply line under a basement floor, an in-floor heating loop, and the buried service outside.

Freeze–thaw finds the weak joint

Calgary swings from a chinook to −30 °C inside a fortnight, and it's the repeated expansion, not the steady cold, that opens a marginal joint. The pipes that fail sit in barely-heated space — garage walls, crawl spaces, rim joists, the run out to a tap nobody drained in October. A leak surfacing in February often started as a freeze nobody noticed. Frozen and burst pipes →

Your home's era narrows the search

Pre-1960 inner-city homes commonly have galvanized supply and clay tile sewer, which is what mature boulevard roots go looking for. Roughly 1975–1997 may mean Poly-B. Post-2000 is generally PEX and ABS. Calgary's hard water works on valve seats and cartridges besides, which is why weeping shut-offs turn up earlier here than the manufacturer expected. Give a plumber your build year on the phone — it tells them where to look. What hard water does to Calgary plumbing →

What it costs, honestly

We read the Calgary pages currently ranking for leak detection in August 2026. Not one publishes a price for it. There's no authoritative public source either — no City department, trade association or consumer body tracks what it costs here. A confident average almost always comes from an American market where plumbing, housing and labour rates all differ.

What does exist are hourly rates Calgary companies publish about themselves: independents at $85–$120 an hour, companies at $95–$150, after-hours at $150–$250. A planning range for diagnostic time and nothing more — self-reported, unaudited, and not a quote.

Four questions before you book anyone

  1. "Should I be calling 311 first?" If your symptoms point at the buried service and nobody mentions The City's leak locators, they're either not listening or not telling you.
  2. "What's the diagnosis cost, and what if you don't find it?" Get the number before the van leaves, get the not-found case answered, and ask whether the fee rolls into the repair.
  3. "Which tool are you bringing, and why that one?" A good answer connects your symptom to the method. "We'll isolate and pressure-test first" beats a list of equipment.
  4. "Can I have the finding in writing, with photos?" If real money follows, you want evidence of what was wrong — for a second quote, for your insurer, and for the next owner of the house.
