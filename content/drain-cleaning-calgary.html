Before you call anyone — us included — spend two minutes on the question below. It's the one that changes what you should do in the next hour.

One fixture, or several? This is the whole diagnosis

Short answer: if one fixture is slow and everything else drains normally, you have a local clog in that fixture's trap or branch line. Annoying, cheap, not urgent. If several fixtures are slow at once, or a basement floor drain gurgles or backs up when you run the washing machine or flush an upstairs toilet, the blockage is in your main line — and that is urgent, because the next load of laundry has nowhere to go but your basement floor.

What to try yourself, and what to leave alone

Plenty of single-fixture clogs clear without a plumber, and we'd rather you saved the money.

  • Hot water and a plunger — yes. Start here on almost everything. Fill the sink enough to cover the plunger cup so you're moving water rather than air, block the overflow hole with a wet cloth, and use steady strokes rather than one heroic shove. On a double sink, plug the other side or you'll just push water across.
  • Clean the trap — yes, if you're comfortable. Bucket underneath, unscrew the two slip nuts under the sink, and look at what comes out. On a bathroom sink it's a hairball nine times out of ten. Reassemble hand-tight and run water while you watch for drips.
  • Pull the pop-up assembly on a bathroom sink — yes. The rod behind the drain catches hair and it's usually the whole problem.
  • A small drum auger on a kitchen sink — maybe. It can work, but go in through the cleanout or the open trap rather than down the drain opening, and don't force it. A cable driven hard into a bend can punch through a corroded old line, and then you have a leak inside a wall instead of a slow sink.
  • A drain auger down a toilet — use a closet auger, not a cable. A bare cable will scratch the porcelain permanently.
  • Anything on the main line — no. Rented drain machines are heavy, powerful and unforgiving, and the mistakes are expensive. This is the point to call.

Chemical drain cleaners: honestly, no

We're not being precious about this, and we're not saying it to sell you a service call. Here's the actual case against them.

  • They usually don't clear a full blockage. Caustic drain cleaner works by chemically attacking what it can reach and stay in contact with. If the line is fully blocked, the product sits on top of the obstruction in a pipe full of standing water, diluted and going nowhere.
  • What doesn't work sits in your trap. Now there's a slug of caustic liquid parked in the pipe, and it stays there.
  • It can damage older pipe. Repeated use is hard on aging cast iron and on the solvent-welded joints in older ABS — and a great deal of Calgary's housing stock is exactly that vintage. Turning a slow drain into a leaking one is a bad trade.
  • It's genuinely hazardous for whoever opens that line next. Whether that's a plumber pulling the trap or you doing it yourself an hour later, caustic liquid under pressure coming back at your face is a real injury. If you've already poured something in, say so when you call. Nobody will judge you; everybody would rather know.
  • Enzyme products are a different thing. Slow-acting biological products used as maintenance on a drain that still flows are a reasonable habit. They will not rescue a blocked line tonight.

A drain that keeps clogging is a symptom, not bad luck

If you're clearing the same drain twice a year, the clog isn't the problem — it's the evidence. Something about that line makes it collect debris, and until that changes you're buying the same service call on a schedule. The usual causes in Calgary homes:

Grease buildup

Cooled fat lines the pipe wall and narrows it a little more each month, then catches everything else that goes past. Classic on kitchen branches, especially long horizontal runs in older houses.

A belly in the line

A section that has settled and now holds standing water instead of draining through. Solids stop there every time. Common where clay or older ABS was laid over backfill that has settled since — and no amount of augering fixes a shape problem.

Root intrusion

Fine roots find a joint, get in, and grow into a mat that catches paper and grease. Cutting them out works and buys you a couple of years; the roots come back because the opening is still there.

A venting problem

Drains need air behind the water to flow properly. A blocked or frost-capped vent stack makes drains gurgle, run slow and pull traps dry, which is also where basement sewer smells come from. Frost-closed vents are a real Calgary phenomenon in deep cold.

Sorting the cause is the difference between clearing a drain and fixing a drain. That's what a camera is for.

When a camera inspection is worth paying for — and when it isn't

Worth it: the same line has blocked more than once; the blockage is in the main and you want to know why before you agree to anything bigger; the house is pre-1970 and you've never seen the sewer's condition; you're planning a basement development or a basement bathroom and want to know what you're tying into; or somebody has recommended a line replacement and you want evidence rather than an opinion. A camera turns "your line is bad" into "there's a root mass at 9 metres and a belly at 14," which is a conversation you can actually make decisions from — and it tells you whether the defect sits on your side of the property line or the City's.

Not worth it: a bathroom sink that's blocked for the first time in ten years. That's a hairball. Pay for a camera when you're deciding something, not to confirm that hair blocks a sink.

If you're buying a house rather than fixing one, the same tool has a different job — see sewer scope before you buy. When you do book an inspection, ask up front whether you get a recorded file and a written report with distances, or just a verbal opinion. Only one of those is useful in a negotiation.

Snaking versus hydro jetting, in plain terms

Snaking (augering) pushes a rotating steel cable down the line with a cutting head on the end. It punches through the blockage and, with the right head, cuts roots out. It's fast, it's cheaper, and it restores flow — which is exactly what you want when there's water on the floor tonight. What it doesn't do is clean the pipe wall. It bores a hole through the grease rather than removing it, so the line re-narrows and you're back in a year.

Hydro jetting runs a high-pressure hose with a rear-facing nozzle down the line and scours the walls, flushing debris back toward the cleanout. On a grease-lined kitchen main it genuinely restores the pipe to something close to its original diameter, so the interval between problems gets much longer rather than slightly longer. It costs more and it takes longer.

The honest tradeoff: jetting is not always the right answer. On a fragile old line — deteriorated cast iron, cracked clay tile, a pipe already holding together on optimism — high pressure can make a bad situation worse. The responsible sequence is to clear the blockage first, camera the line second, and only then decide whether jetting is appropriate. Anyone quoting hydro jetting on a pre-1960 line they've never seen is guessing with your pipe.

Grease, roots and the Calgary specifics

The kitchen sink

Everything you pour down warm arrives cool. Bacon fat, the oil off a roasting pan, the film off dishes — it congeals on the pipe wall a few metres along where the line has cooled, and it builds. Long horizontal runs in older homes and unheated crawl spaces make it worse, because the pipe is colder and the fat sets faster. A garburator doesn't help; it adds ground organic matter for the grease to hold onto. Scrape plates into the bin, wipe pans before washing, and pour cooking fat into a tin and throw it out.

Roots, boulevard trees and clay tile

Root intrusion in Calgary has a specific profile. Inner-city and older established neighbourhoods have mature boulevard trees — elm, poplar, ash — with root systems that reach far past their canopy looking for water and nutrients. Homes built before roughly 1970 typically have clay tile sewer lines built in short sections with mortared joints. Those joints shift with our frost cycles and open slightly, warm moist sewer air escapes, and the roots go straight to it. It isn't the tree's fault and it isn't yours; it's the combination.

If your house fits that description, the useful posture is to know the condition rather than wait to find out at 11 p.m. Preventive augering on a known root line every year or two is far cheaper than an emergency backup and a basement cleanup. And when a line is genuinely past saving, a camera survey with distances is what tells you whether the failure is on your side of the property line or The City's — a distinction that decides who pays, and one we explain in full on the main plumbing page and in our responsibility guide.
