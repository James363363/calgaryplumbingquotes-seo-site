One fixture, or several? This is the whole diagnosis

Short answer: if one fixture is slow and everything else drains normally, you have a local clog in that fixture's trap or branch line — annoying, cheap, not urgent. If several are slow at once, or a basement floor drain gurgles or backs up when the washing machine empties, the blockage is in your main line, and that is urgent: the next load of laundry has nowhere to go but your basement floor.

If it's sewage coming up rather than water going down slowly, call 311 before you call a plumber. It costs nothing and it's the only way to find out whether the problem is your line or a surcharged City main — and if it's the main, half your block is backing up and augering your pipe accomplishes nothing. Know the limits, though: City crews will not move furniture, pull back carpet, remove toilets or open drywall to find the source, and if it's on your side they'll tell you to call a plumber.

If your basement has taken sewage more than once, clearing the line is the emergency fix, not the long-term one — a backwater valve is what stops the street's problem becoming your floor's, and water seeping up around the slab rather than out of a drain belongs with a sump pump instead. One winter variation: a main that backs up during a deep cold snap in a house where nothing has changed may be a frozen sewer line rather than a blockage. It looks identical from inside; the fix is completely different.

What to try yourself, and what to leave alone

Plenty of single-fixture clogs clear without a plumber, and we'd rather you saved the money. Nothing here puts you offside, either: the City does not require a plumbing permit to remove a blockage in the drainage, repair a leak, or replace existing faucets and fixtures.

Source: The City of Calgary — homeowner electrical and plumbing permits (checked August 2026). Where the line falls on bigger jobs: DIY plumbing and the law in Alberta.

  • Hot water and a plunger — yes. Cover the cup so you're moving water rather than air, block the overflow with a wet cloth, and use steady strokes rather than one heroic shove. On a double sink, plug the other side or you'll just push water across.
  • Clean the trap — yes, if you're comfortable. Bucket underneath, unscrew the two slip nuts, and look at what comes out; on a bathroom sink it's a hairball nine times out of ten. Reassemble hand-tight and watch for drips.
  • Pull the pop-up assembly on a bathroom sink — yes. The rod behind the drain catches hair and is usually the whole problem.
  • A small drum auger on a kitchen sink — maybe. Go in through the cleanout or the open trap, not the drain opening, and don't force it — a cable driven hard into a bend can punch through a corroded old line, and then you have a leak inside a wall instead of a slow sink.
  • A toilet — closet auger, not a bare cable, which scratches the porcelain permanently.
  • Anything on the main line — no. Rented drain machines are heavy, powerful and unforgiving. This is the point to call.

Chemical drain cleaners: honestly, no

Caustic drain cleaner only attacks what it can stay in contact with, so on a fully blocked line it sits on top of the obstruction in a pipe full of standing water, diluted and going nowhere — and what doesn't work stays parked in your trap. Repeated use is hard on aging cast iron and on the solvent-welded joints in older ABS, a great deal of Calgary's housing stock, and it is hazardous for whoever opens that line next: a plumber, or you an hour later. If you've already poured something in, say so when you call; everybody would rather know.

Enzyme products are a different thing: slow-acting biological maintenance on a drain that still flows is a reasonable habit, but it will not rescue a blocked line tonight.

A drain that keeps clogging is a symptom, not bad luck

If you're clearing the same drain twice a year, the clog isn't the problem — it's the evidence. Something about that line collects debris, and until that changes you're buying the same service call on a schedule. The usual causes in Calgary homes:

Grease buildup

Cooled fat lines the pipe wall and narrows it a little more each month, then catches everything that goes past. Classic on long kitchen branches in older houses.

A belly in the line

A settled section that holds standing water instead of draining through, so solids stop there every time. No amount of augering fixes a shape problem.

Root intrusion

Fine roots find a joint and grow into a mat that catches paper and grease. Cutting them out buys a couple of years; they come back, because the opening is still there.

A venting problem

Drains need air behind the water to flow. A blocked or frost-capped vent stack makes them gurgle, run slow and pull traps dry — which is where basement sewer smells come from.

When a camera inspection is worth paying for — and when it isn't

Worth it: the same line has blocked more than once; the blockage is in the main and you want to know why before agreeing to anything bigger; the house is pre-1970 and you've never seen the sewer's condition; you're planning a basement bathroom and want to know what you're tying into; or someone has recommended a line replacement and you want evidence rather than an opinion. A camera turns "your line is bad" into "root mass at 9 metres, belly at 14" — a conversation you can make decisions from.

Not worth it: a bathroom sink blocked for the first time in ten years. That's a hairball. Pay for a camera when you're deciding something.

If you're buying a house rather than fixing one, the same tool has a different job — see sewer scope before you buy. Either way, ask up front whether you get a recorded file and distances or just a verbal opinion. Only one is useful in a negotiation.

Snaking versus hydro jetting, in plain terms

Snaking (augering) drives a rotating steel cable down the line with a cutting head on the end, punching through the blockage and, with the right head, cutting roots out. It's fast and cheap and it restores flow — what you want when there's water on the floor tonight. What it doesn't do is clean the pipe wall, so the line re-narrows and you're back in a year.

Hydro jetting runs a high-pressure hose with a rear-facing nozzle down the line and scours the walls, flushing debris back toward the cleanout. On a grease-lined kitchen main that restores something close to the original diameter, so the interval between problems gets much longer rather than slightly longer. It costs more and takes longer.

The honest tradeoff: on a fragile old line — deteriorated cast iron, cracked clay tile, a pipe holding together on optimism — high pressure can make things worse. Clear it, camera it, then decide. Anyone quoting jetting on a pre-1960 line they've never seen is guessing with your pipe.

Grease, roots and the Calgary specifics

The kitchen sink

Everything you pour down warm arrives cool. Bacon fat, the oil off a roasting pan, the film off dishes — it congeals on the pipe wall a few metres along, and an unheated crawl space makes it worse because the fat sets faster. A garburator doesn't help; it adds ground organic matter for the grease to hold onto. Scrape plates into the bin, wipe pans before washing, and pour cooking fat into a tin.

Roots, boulevard trees and clay tile

Root intrusion here has a specific profile. Established neighbourhoods have mature boulevard trees — elm, poplar, ash — whose roots range far past the canopy looking for water, and homes built before roughly 1970 typically have clay tile sewer lines laid in short sections with mortared joints. Frost cycling nudges those joints open, warm moist sewer air escapes, and the roots go straight to it. On a house like that, preventive augering every year or two is far cheaper than an emergency backup and a basement cleanup — and when a line is past saving, a camera survey with distances tells you whether the failure sits on your side of the property line or The City's, the distinction that decides who pays.
