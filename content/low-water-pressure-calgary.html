Short answer: before anything else, answer one question — did the pressure drop suddenly, or has it faded over years? A sudden drop is a valve, a failed pressure reducing valve, a leak, or something happening on the City's side of the property line. A slow fade over five or ten years is almost always your own pipes closing up from the inside, which in Calgary usually means galvanized steel supply lines in a pre-1960 house. Those two answers lead to completely different repairs and completely different bills.

Almost every article on this topic gives you the same undifferentiated list of nine possible causes and leaves you to sort it out. The sudden-versus-gradual split does most of the sorting for you in about ten seconds, so start there and work down whichever branch you're on.

It dropped suddenly

Something changed. Work outward from the cheapest and most likely.

  1. Ask a neighbour. Seriously, first. If their pressure is down too, it isn't your plumbing — it's the distribution system, and no plumber can help you. Crews work on mains, hydrants get flushed, a break upstream drops a whole run of houses. Call 311 and save yourself a service call.
  2. Check your main shut-off is fully open. In most Calgary homes it's in the basement on the wall facing the street, often near the meter and within sight of the water heater. A gate valve that got closed a couple of turns during a furnace service, a renovation, or the last time somebody fixed a tap is one of the most common causes of a sudden drop, and it's a free fix. Quarter-turn ball valves should be in line with the pipe; round handle valves should be wound fully open, then backed off a quarter turn.
  3. Check the pressure reducing valve, if you have one. Not every house does. It's a bell-shaped brass fitting on the incoming line, usually within a metre of where the pipe enters the basement, with an adjusting screw and locknut on top. PRVs fail, and the common failure mode drops your pressure across the whole house and keeps it down. It's a replaceable part, not a house-wide problem.
  4. Rule out a leak. Shut off every tap and appliance, then watch the flow indicator on your water meter. If it's still turning, water is going somewhere — possibly the buried service line between your property line and the house, which can pull pressure down without ever showing itself indoors.
  5. Check whether it's hot only, cold only, or both. Weak hot water alone points at the water heater's shut-off, a partially closed valve on the tank inlet, or sediment — not your supply. Weak on both sides means it's upstream of the tank.

Leave the curb stop alone

The valve under the small metal cap out near your sidewalk is the water service valve. The City of Calgary owns it, and neither you nor your plumber may legally operate it — it sits essentially at the property line, on the City's side of the split. If you suspect it's partly closed, that's a 311 call. Prying the cap off and having a go is how people turn a pressure complaint into a City repair bill.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service lines. More on who owns which pipe.

It's faded over years

Nobody notices this one until they stay somewhere else and remember what a shower is supposed to feel like. Gradual loss means the inside diameter of your pipes is shrinking, and there are two mechanisms doing it.

Galvanized steel, closing from the inside

Calgary grew in distinct waves and each one left a plumbing signature. Inner-city homes built before roughly 1960 — the older parts of the northwest and southwest, the established communities close to downtown — frequently still have galvanized steel supply lines. Galvanized rusts internally, and the corrosion builds inward in layers rather than eating outward. A three-quarter-inch pipe can end up with a quarter-inch of usable opening while looking completely intact from the outside.

The tells, beyond the slow fade: rusty or brown water from both hot and cold taps, worst first thing in the morning or after a week away; pressure that collapses when a second fixture is opened; and threaded joints that look furred and crusty where they meet. If the hot water is discoloured but the cold runs clear, that's the water heater rather than your piping — that's a tank-age question.

What fixing it involves is worth understanding before anyone quotes it. Repiping means running new copper or PEX from the main to each fixture group, which means opening walls and ceilings along those runs, and it's usually done in phases — the main trunk and the bathroom first, kitchen and laundry after. Two honest points: a partial repipe that leaves galvanized branches in place gives you a partial result, and drywall repair and paint are frequently quoted separately, so ask specifically whether they're in the number. This is a plumbing-system alteration, which as of August 2026 means a plumbing permit is required.

Source: The City of Calgary — Plumbing and gas permits. See also what you can legally do yourself in Alberta.

Hardness scale at the fixtures

The second mechanism is our water. Calgary's supply ran roughly 141–200 mg/L of calcium carbonate from the Bearspaw plant and 181–274 mg/L from Glenmore across 2025 — about 8–12 and 11–16 grains per gallon respectively, converting at 17.1 mg/L per grain. That's hard to very hard, and it builds up wherever water passes through a small opening: aerator screens, shower heads, mixing cartridges, appliance inlet screens and solenoid valves.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water quality and water hardness data. Conversion to gpg is ours. Full seasonal figures for both plants →

What scale does not generally do is close up modern copper or PEX supply lines the way people imagine. If your house was built after about 1970 and the pressure has faded everywhere, look harder at the PRV and at a partly-closed valve before you accept a repiping quote.

One fixture or the whole house?

The other split that saves you money. Test it properly: run the kitchen cold, then a bathroom cold, then an outdoor tap, and note which are weak.

Pressure and flow are not the same thing, and conflating them sends people down the wrong path. Pressure is the force behind the water; flow is how much actually arrives per minute. A restricted pipe can have perfectly normal pressure sitting behind it and still deliver a miserable shower, which is exactly why a corroded galvanized line feels like "low pressure" to everyone who has one. When a plumber puts a gauge on your hose bib, they're measuring pressure — but the problem you're describing is usually flow.

When low pressure is really a leak

This is the one that costs you twice, because you're getting a worse shower and paying for water you never used. Suspect a leak when the drop had no obvious trigger, when you can hear running water with everything shut off, when there's a damp or unusually warm patch on a basement floor, or when the water bill climbed before you noticed the pressure.

The City's own benchmark is a useful gut-check on scale: a slow leak that takes a minute to fill a 500 mL pop bottle can add over twenty cubic metres a month. At 2026 residential rates of $1.7409 per m³ for water and $1.9050 per m³ for wastewater, twenty cubic metres works out to about $72.92 a month, roughly $875 a year — that's our arithmetic on The City's figures, not a number The City publishes.

Sources: The City of Calgary — Water leaks and basic repairs and residential water rates. How to actually find it →

If the leak turns out to be on the buried service line between your property line and the house, two things matter. That portion is yours — you pay all repair costs on private property, with the water meter as the only exception — and under the Water Utility Bylaw an owner must repair a confirmed service line leak within 15 days or The City may issue a remedial order. So it isn't a job that can drift.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service line leaks.

What we'd actually check, in order

  • Neighbours first. Free, thirty seconds, and it rules out the entire category of problems no plumber can bill you for.
  • Then the aerators. If it's one fixture, you've solved it for the price of a bottle of vinegar — and in this city aerators clog fast enough that this is worth doing on a schedule anyway.
  • Then the main shut-off. Fully open, both the main and any valve on the line feeding the affected part of the house. Free.
  • Then the meter test. Everything off, watch the flow indicator. Catches hidden leaks before they show up as a bill. Free.
  • Then a gauge reading. A plumber screws a gauge onto an outdoor tap and reads static pressure in a few minutes. That's what tells you whether a PRV has failed, and it's a cheap diagnostic compared to guessing.
  • Only then, the pipe conversation. Repiping is the right answer for a lot of pre-1960 inner-city homes, but it should be the conclusion of a diagnosis, never the opening pitch.

Rates in this city are worth a word too. Plumbing companies around Calgary publish figures of roughly $85–$120 an hour for independents and $95–$150 for companies, with after-hours in the $150–$250 range. Those are self-reported company numbers — there is no authoritative public source for Calgary plumber rates, and anyone presenting one as official is overstating it. Low pressure is essentially never an emergency, so there's no reason to pay the after-hours column for it.
