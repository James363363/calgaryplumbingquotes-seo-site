Water goes into the house fine. It just won't leave. The toilet fills and stalls, the tub gurgles when the washing machine drains, and eventually something comes up onto the basement floor — all in the middle of a January cold snap. That combination usually means one thing, and it isn't a hairball in the trap.

Short answer: A frozen sewer line is ice forming inside your drain line, usually in the buried run between the house and the property line. The tell is simple — you still have water coming in at every tap, but multiple fixtures on the lowest floor stop draining at once. It needs to be thawed mechanically, it's a same-day call in a cold snap because everything you run goes back onto your floor, and if it happens two winters running the pipe itself has a problem that thawing will never fix.

Frozen sewer line or frozen water pipe? Tell them apart first

These get confused constantly and the fixes have nothing in common. Your house has two separate pipe systems crossing the property line: a pressurised water service bringing clean water in, and a gravity sewer taking waste out. Either can freeze, and the symptoms are opposites.

Stop using water while you work out which it is. Every load of laundry, every flush, every shower on a blocked sewer line is water that has nowhere to go except back up through the lowest opening in your house — typically the basement floor drain or a basement shower. People routinely turn a manageable problem into a sewage cleanup by running the dishwasher to "test it."

Why sewer lines freeze in Calgary specifically

A sewer line is not pressurised and it is not full. It's a pipe with a shallow stream of warm wastewater running along the bottom a few times a day, and that warm flow is most of what keeps it clear in winter. Take the flow away, or let the line hold standing water, and ice builds from the bottom up until the pipe closes.

Not enough flow going down it

The biggest one. A house that's empty, mid-renovation, between tenants, or whose single occupant is away for the holidays sends almost nothing down the line for days. Nothing warm passes through, frost keeps driving down from above, and the pipe closes from the inside. It's why the calls cluster in early January.

The line sits higher than the frost

Bury depth varies enormously across this city depending on when the house was built, whether the lot was re-graded, and whether a basement development or addition changed the run. A section that's shallow somewhere along its length — often right where it leaves the foundation, or under a driveway — is the part that goes first.

Sag, belly or bad slope holding standing water

A drain line is supposed to fall continuously toward the main. Clay tile that has shifted, a section that settled as Calgary clay soil moved with the moisture cycle, or a badly bedded repair leaves a low spot — a "belly" — holding water permanently instead of draining. Standing water in a cold pipe is exactly what freezes, and it's the most common reason a line goes every single year.

A cold, dry winter with no snow on the ground

Counterintuitive but important: snow is insulation. A snowpack on the lawn and boulevard holds the frost line up, so a cold snap in a dry winter with bare brown grass drives frost noticeably deeper than the same temperatures with a foot of snow on the ground. The dry cold winters are when frozen sewer calls spike. Same logic under a driveway or sidewalk that's shovelled clean all winter — you've removed the blanket over that section.

Why it's usually the same houses every year

Ask anyone who does drain work in this city: frozen sewer lines aren't random. It's a small set of addresses and it's the same ones each winter, which is the useful diagnostic. Freezing once in a brutal cold snap in an empty house is bad luck. Freezing in consecutive winters, or freezing when the neighbours' don't, is telling you something structural about the pipe — it's shallow, it has a belly holding water, it's partly collapsed, or roots and scale have narrowed it enough that flow can't clear it.

Here's the part most companies won't say out loud, because it costs them a repeat winter service call: thawing does not fix any of that. Thawing restores flow for this week. If the underlying reason the ice formed is a slope or depth problem, the pipe will close again on the next cold snap, and you'll pay again, probably at an after-hours rate, probably in the same month.

  • Camera it once it's thawed. A camera run shows standing water, sags, cracked or offset clay tile, root intrusion and where along the run the trouble sits — so the repair decision comes from evidence instead of a guess.
  • Ask for the footage and the distance markers. A camera report that says "roots at 11 metres, standing water 14 to 17 metres" is something you can get a second quote against. "It's bad, needs replacing" is not.
  • Locate before you dig. A camera with a sonde marks the exact spot on the ground, which usually means a targeted excavation rather than trenching the whole front yard.

Buying an older Calgary house? This is the argument for a sewer scope before you close. A belly in the line is invisible to a home inspector and expensive to inherit.

How a frozen sewer line actually gets thawed

Not with a hair dryer. The ice is in a buried pipe, usually five to twenty metres out from the foundation, so there's no way to put heat on the outside of it. It gets thawed from the inside, through a cleanout, with a steam unit or hot-water jetting fed down the line until it eats through the plug.

Two reasons that isn't DIY. The equipment is genuinely dangerous — live steam in a confined pipe with someone's hands at the cleanout — and it isn't something you rent for an afternoon. And electric thawing, running current through a metal pipe to heat it, should have died out entirely: it does nothing on plastic or clay, it has caused fires, and modern lines are often a mix of materials, so nobody knows what the current will do.

Do not pour anything down the drain to "melt" it. Not boiling water in volume, not salt, not chemical drain cleaner, not antifreeze. Boiling water hits the ice plug, doesn't clear it, and now you have scalding water sitting in a pipe that's already backing up toward your basement. Chemical drain cleaner sitting in a blocked line is a hazard for whoever opens the cleanout next — tell your plumber if you've already used some.

Expect the visit to involve locating and opening a cleanout, thawing until flow returns, then running a good volume of water to confirm the line is genuinely clear rather than weeping through a pinhole in the ice. Ask for a camera run at the same visit while the gear is already on site. Is it your line or the City's?

Same boundary as everything else: The City owns the pipe from the main to your property line, and the homeowner is responsible for the water and sewer service connections from the property line to the house. That line typically sits only a few metres in from the curb, so most of the buried run across your front yard is yours.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water and sewer connections

Call 311 anyway when sewage is backing up. The City's published sewer backup procedure has an agent determine whether the problem is your internal plumbing or possibly City infrastructure, which is worth establishing before you pay anyone to dig. Two things to know going in: City crews "will not move furniture, pull back carpet, remove toilets or make holes in drywall to identify the source of the backup," and if the blockage is on private property you'll be advised to call a plumber.

Source: The City of Calgary — Sewage backup

One signal worth passing on to 311: if neighbours on your block are backing up the same night, that points at the main. If it's only your house, it's very likely yours. Full ownership breakdown and what to say when you call is on our who-owns-which-pipe page.

Preventing it next winter

  • Keep water moving through a vacant property. Have someone run a hot tap and flush the toilets on a real schedule, or get the plumbing properly winterized and drained. Warm flow is the cheapest protection there is.
  • Don't let the house go cold. The City's 15 °C minimum indoor guidance exists to protect the water service, and it helps here too — a warm basement keeps the first stretch of drain line warm.
  • Insulate exposed cleanouts and any drain running through unheated space. A cleanout stub in a cold crawl space or an unheated garage is a direct path for cold into the line.
  • Leave snow where it insulates. You don't need to shovel the lawn or the boulevard above the sewer run. Snow cover holds the frost line up.
  • Fix the line, don't re-thaw it annually. Frozen twice? Budget for the repair the camera identifies. Two after-hours thaws and a sewage cleanup add up fast.
  • Know where your cleanout is before December. Behind boxes in the crawl space or buried under a deck? Find it now — hunting for it at 11 p.m. is time on the clock.

And if your basement has backed up before, frozen or not, that's the backwater valve conversation. It won't stop your line freezing, but it stops what comes back the other way.
