Water goes into the house fine. It just won't leave. The toilet fills and stalls, the tub gurgles when the washing machine drains, and eventually something comes up onto the basement floor — in the middle of a January cold snap. That combination isn't a hairball in the trap.

Short answer: A frozen sewer line is ice forming inside your drain line, usually in the buried run between the house and the property line. The tell is simple — you still have water coming in at every tap, but multiple fixtures on the lowest floor stop draining at once. It needs to be thawed mechanically, it's a same-day call in a cold snap because everything you run goes back onto your floor, and if it happens two winters running the pipe itself has a problem that thawing will never fix.

Frozen sewer line or frozen water pipe? Tell them apart first

These get confused constantly, and the fixes have nothing in common. Two separate pipe systems cross your property line: a pressurised water service bringing clean water in, and a gravity sewer taking waste out. Either can freeze, and the symptoms are opposites.

Stop using water while you work out which it is. Every flush and every load of laundry on a blocked sewer line has nowhere to go but back up through the lowest opening in the house — usually the basement floor drain. People routinely turn a manageable problem into a sewage cleanup by running the dishwasher to "test it."

Why sewer lines freeze in Calgary specifically

A sewer line is not pressurised and it is not full. It's a pipe with a shallow stream of warm wastewater running along the bottom a few times a day, and that warm flow is most of what keeps it clear in winter. Take the flow away, or let the line hold standing water, and ice builds from the bottom up until the pipe closes.

Not enough flow going down it

The biggest one. A house that's empty, mid-renovation, between tenants, or whose occupant is away for the holidays sends almost nothing down the line for days. Nothing warm passes through, frost keeps driving down from above, and the pipe closes from the inside. It's why the calls cluster in early January.

The line sits higher than the frost

Bury depth varies enormously across this city depending on when the house was built, whether the lot was re-graded, and whether a basement development or addition changed the run. A shallow section — often right where it leaves the foundation, or under a driveway — is the part that goes first.

Sag, belly or bad slope holding standing water

A drain line is supposed to fall continuously toward the main. Clay tile that has shifted, a section that settled as Calgary clay soil moved with the moisture cycle, or a badly bedded repair leaves a low spot — a "belly" — holding water permanently. Standing water in a cold pipe is exactly what freezes, and it's the most common reason a line goes every year.

A cold, dry winter with no snow on the ground

Counterintuitive but important: snow is insulation. A snowpack on the lawn and boulevard holds the frost line up, so a cold snap in a dry winter with bare brown grass drives frost noticeably deeper than the same temperatures with a foot of snow down. Dry cold winters are when these calls spike — and a driveway shovelled clean all season is the same effect over that stretch of pipe.

Why it's usually the same houses every year

Frozen sewer lines aren't random — it's a small set of addresses, and largely the same ones each winter. That pattern is the useful diagnostic. Freezing in consecutive winters, or freezing when the neighbours' lines don't, is the pipe telling you something structural: it's shallow, it has a belly holding water, it's partly collapsed, or roots and scale have narrowed it enough that flow can't clear it.

Here's the part most companies won't say out loud, because it costs them a repeat winter service call: thawing does not fix any of that. It restores flow for this week. If the reason the ice formed is a slope or depth problem, the pipe closes again on the next cold snap — and you pay again, probably at an after-hours rate, probably the same month.

  • Camera it once it's thawed. A camera run shows standing water, sags, cracked or offset clay tile, root intrusion and where along the run the trouble sits — so the repair decision comes from evidence instead of a guess.
  • Ask for the footage and the distance markers. A camera report that says "roots at 11 metres, standing water 14 to 17 metres" is something you can get a second quote against. "It's bad, needs replacing" is not.
  • Locate before you dig. A camera with a sonde marks the exact spot on the ground, which usually means a targeted excavation rather than trenching the whole front yard. It's the same principle as locating a leak on the water side — find it first, dig once.

Buying an older Calgary house? This is the argument for a sewer scope before you close — a belly is invisible to a home inspector and expensive to inherit.

How a frozen sewer line actually gets thawed

Not with a hair dryer. The ice is in a buried pipe, usually five to twenty metres out from the foundation, so there's no getting heat to the outside of it. It gets thawed from the inside — through a cleanout, with a steam unit or hot-water jetting fed down the line until it eats through the plug.

That isn't rental gear, and it isn't a DIY job: live steam in a confined pipe with someone's hands at the cleanout is genuinely dangerous. Electric thawing — running current along the pipe to heat it — is worth refusing outright as well. It does nothing on plastic or clay, and a line in an older Calgary house is often a mix of materials, so there's no telling what the current is doing or where.

Do not pour anything down the drain to "melt" it. Not boiling water in volume, not salt, not chemical drain cleaner, not antifreeze. And if you've already tried drain cleaner, say so before anyone opens the cleanout — it's still sitting in there.

Expect the visit to run: locate and open a cleanout, thaw until flow returns, then run a good volume of water to confirm the line is genuinely clear rather than weeping through a pinhole in the ice. Ask for the camera run at the same visit, while the gear is already on site. Is it your line or the City's?

Same boundary as everything else: builders or homeowners are responsible for providing the water and sewer service connections from the property line to the plumbing system, and The City's own wording is that anything installed on private property is owned by the homeowner and built at the homeowner's expense.

Source: The City of Calgary — Installing water and sewer connections

Where that boundary sits decides how much of the buried run is yours. In most cases, The City says, the property line ends a few metres from the edge of the curb or sidewalk — so most of the pipe crossing your front yard is on your side of it.

Source: The City of Calgary — Water service lines in Calgary

Call 311 anyway when sewage is backing up. The City's published sewer backup procedure has an agent determine whether the problem is your internal plumbing or possibly City infrastructure, which is worth establishing before you pay anyone to dig. Two things to know going in: City crews "will not move furniture, pull back carpet, remove toilets or make holes in drywall to identify the source of the backup," and if the blockage is on private property you'll be advised to call a plumber.

Source: The City of Calgary — Sewage backups

One signal worth passing on to 311: if neighbours on your block are backing up the same night, that points at the main. If it's only your house, it's very likely yours. Full ownership breakdown and what to say when you call is on our who-owns-which-pipe page.

Preventing it next winter

  • Keep water moving through a vacant property. Someone running a hot tap and flushing the toilets on a real schedule, or the plumbing properly winterized and drained. Warm flow is the cheapest protection there is.
  • Don't let the house go cold. The City's winter guidance is to "keep your thermostat set for at least 15°C" — that's aimed at the water service, but it helps here too, because a warm basement keeps the first stretch of drain line warm as it leaves the foundation.
  • Insulate exposed cleanouts and any drain running through unheated space. A cleanout stub in a cold crawl space or an unheated garage is a direct path for cold into the line.
  • Leave snow where it insulates. You don't need to shovel the lawn or the boulevard above the sewer run. Snow cover holds the frost line up.
  • Fix the line, don't re-thaw it annually. Frozen twice? Budget for the repair the camera identifies.
  • Know where your cleanout is before December. Behind boxes in the crawl space or buried under a deck? Find it now — hunting for it at 11 p.m. is time on the clock.

Thermostat guidance: The City of Calgary — Protect your water lines and meter from freezing. That page covers the water service, not the sewer; The City publishes no equivalent frozen-sewer guidance.
