The first five minutes matter more than our drive time

Whatever the response time turns out to be, what you do before anyone arrives determines how much this costs. In that order:

  1. Shut off the water at the main. Basement, low on the street-facing wall, usually near the meter and the hot water tank. Clockwise on a round handle, quarter turn on a lever. If it's one fixture only, there's a smaller stop valve right at it and you can isolate without shutting the house down. You cannot operate the curb stop at the property line — that valve is The City's and only a City crew can turn it.
  2. Kill the power to wet areas. Breakers off before you walk into standing water in a finished basement. If the panel is in the flooded area, stay out and call the utility.
  3. Shut the gas control off if it's the water heater. A gas tank that's lost its water and keeps firing is a worse problem than a leaking one. Gas control knob to off, then close the cold water inlet valve on top of the tank.
  4. Photograph everything before you clean up. Wide shots of the room, close-ups of the failed part, the water line on the wall, the soaked contents. The most valuable unpaid five minutes in the whole event — an adjuster assesses what they can see, and a floor you've already mopped proves nothing.
  5. Then start moving water. Wet vac, towels, fans, contents up off the floor. Speed beats thoroughness — standing water wicks into drywall while you decide.

If it's a frozen or burst pipe, the full thaw-versus-burst decision tree is on our frozen and burst pipes page. If it's drains backing up in a cold snap, check whether you've got a frozen sewer line before anyone augers anything.

How fast can anyone really get here?

On an ordinary Tuesday, same-day is realistic and a couple of hours is common. In the week a cold snap breaks — it hits −30 °C, then chinooks back above freezing — every plumbing company in this city is triaging, and anyone giving you a hard number that week is guessing.

An honest answer sounds like: "Three ahead of you, realistically late afternoon, here's what to do meanwhile, I'll call if that changes." A dishonest one is a confident 60-minute promise from a call centre with no idea where the vans are. Judge a company on whether they'll tell you something you don't want to hear — it's the same quality you're relying on when they tell you what a repair does and doesn't need.

Same on the City side. If sewage is backing up, call 311 too — the agent works out whether it looks like your internal plumbing or possibly City infrastructure. Note that City crews will not move furniture, pull back carpet, remove toilets or make holes in drywall to find the source, and if the blockage is on private property you'll be told to call a plumber.

How after-hours pricing actually works

There is no authoritative public source for Calgary plumber rates. No industry survey, no City regulation of pricing, and every figure online is a company's self-report about itself. Anyone quoting a precise city-wide average is making it look more solid than it is.

For scale rather than accuracy: one Calgary company publishes roughly $85–$120 an hour for independent plumbers, $95–$150 for plumbing companies, and $150–$250 for emergency and after-hours work. One company's self-reported view, not a benchmark.

Source: Mr. Mike's Plumbing — What do plumbers charge per hour in Calgary (updated December 2025). Self-reported; not an independent survey.

What varies more than the hourly rate is the structure around it, and that's what to ask about on the phone:

  • Is there a call-out or trip charge separate from labour? This is where after-hours pricing hides. Ask for the number, not "there may be a fee."
  • Is there a minimum billable time? One hour, two, or a flat after-hours minimum — it changes the bill for a twenty-minute fix a lot.
  • When does the after-hours rate start and stop? Evenings, weekends and statutory holidays are often priced differently from each other.
  • Am I paying for diagnosis, and does it come off the repair? Finding a hidden leak or camera-ing a line is real work and gets billed. Reasonable is knowing the number first and having it credited if you proceed.
  • What do I owe if I decide not to go ahead? Ask this one explicitly. The trap around this city is the "free estimate" that becomes a service call charge once the van is in the driveway.
  • Is it quoted or hourly? If the job can be scoped on arrival, a fixed price protects you from the clock. If it genuinely can't be, hourly is honest — but you should be told which before work starts.

What to have ready when you call

Two minutes of prep cuts a lot of guessing out of the visit, and sometimes tells the person on the phone you don't need one at all.

What's happening, specifically

Where it's coming from, clean or foul, how fast, how long. "Wet ceiling under the upstairs bathroom, started an hour ago, spreading" is worth ten times "there's a leak."

What you've already done

Main off or on. Breakers off. Fixtures isolated. Anything you've poured down a drain — especially chemical cleaner, which matters for whoever opens that line.

What the house is

Age and neighbourhood, finished or unfinished basement, whether there's a suite, whether it's happened before. A 1958 bungalow and a 2015 build fail in completely different ways.

Access

Where to park, lane or front, whether the mechanical room is behind stored boxes, whether there's a dog. Sounds trivial; saves real minutes at 2 a.m.

Photos, if you can

A photo of the failed part texted ahead often means the right materials are on the van the first time — one visit instead of two.

Your questions about price

Call-out fee, minimum time, what you owe if you don't proceed. Ask before someone drives out, not after — that's when you have the choice.
