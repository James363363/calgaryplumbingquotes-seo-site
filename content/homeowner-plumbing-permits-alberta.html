Short answer: Yes, you can legally do plumbing work in your own Calgary home — even though plumber is a compulsory certification trade in Alberta. The trade rules carry an owner exemption for property you own and intend "only for personal and not-for-profit use." That exemption settles who may hold the wrench. It does not excuse you from the City of Calgary's permit and inspection rules, and it does not extend to gas work, where a homeowner is effectively locked out entirely.

Almost every page on this question gets one half and misses the other. Some say plumbing is a restricted trade in Alberta and stop there, leaving you thinking a licensed plumber has to change your kitchen faucet. Others say homeowners can do whatever they like on their own house, which is how people end up with an unpermitted basement bathroom that torpedoes a sale eight years later. Both halves are true at once.

The two rules that look like a contradiction

Rule one: plumber is a compulsory trade

Alberta divides its trades into optional and compulsory certification. Plumbing is compulsory. That means the restricted activities of the trade may only be performed by someone holding a journeyperson certificate, a recognized equivalent trade certificate, or a registered apprentice working toward one. It is not a professional courtesy or an industry preference — it's the licensing framework, and it's why the person who shows up from any legitimate company can name their ticket when you ask.

For scale: the apprenticeship runs four periods, each requiring 1,560 hours of on-the-job experience plus eight weeks of classroom instruction.

Source: Alberta Trade Secrets — Plumber trade profile

Rule two: there is an explicit owner exemption

The same regulatory framework lists classes of individuals who may perform those restricted activities without the certificate. One of them is the owner. In the province's own wording, the restricted activities may be performed by any individual who is performing the restricted activity "in respect of any property that the individual owns, has possession of or control over, and intends only for personal and not-for-profit use by the individual."

Source: Alberta Trade Secrets — Classes of individuals, Plumber

Reading them together

There's no contradiction once you see what each rule is aimed at. The compulsory designation regulates selling plumbing work to other people. The exemption removes you from that restriction when the property is yours and the benefit is yours. You cannot plumb your neighbour's house for cash. You can plumb your own.

So the real question was never "am I allowed?" It's "what does the City need to see?" — a separate authority with separate rules.

The City's homeowner permit is a second, stricter test

The City of Calgary issues a homeowner plumbing permit, but only if you clear all three of these:

  • You own the home.
  • You live in it, or intend to reside in it. Rental property does not qualify.
  • You are doing the work yourself. You cannot pull a permit on a contractor's behalf, and no, this is not a loophole for hiring your cousin.

Two structural exclusions catch people out: the permit isn't available for apartment-style condominiums at all, and an individual rowhouse or townhouse unit needs condo board authorization first.

The fee is $116.50 — a $112 permit fee plus a $4.50 Safety Codes Council levy, with no processing fee on top. Fees increase annually on January 1, so confirm the current number when you apply rather than trusting this page in three years. For comparison, a contractor pulling a trade permit pays $112 processing plus $9.79 per $1,000 of construction value, which is part of why a permitted job quoted by a company costs more than the sticker on the parts.

Source: The City of Calgary — Homeowner electrical and plumbing permits

What you're buying for $116.50 isn't permission. It's a qualified inspector looking at your rough-in before it disappears behind drywall, and a record that the work exists and passed — which is what protects you at resale and gives you something to point at if a claim ever gets awkward.

What needs a permit, and what genuinely doesn't

The City's test is whether the plumbing system is being constructed, extended or altered. Changing what hangs off an existing connection is not the same as changing the system.

The two most common real-world questions land on opposite sides of that line. A like-for-like hot water tank swap looks a lot like a fixture replacement — but the gas and venting attached to it are what pull it back into permit territory. And a basement bathroom is an extension of the system every time, no exceptions.

Gas is where the exemption runs out

A Calgary homeowner cannot pull a gas permit. The only exception is a homeowner who personally holds Alberta first- or second-class gasfitter certification, resides or intends to reside at the work location, and applies in person with proof of qualifications. For everyone else, a licensed contractor must take out the permit and do the work.

There is one narrow carve-out worth knowing: you may pull a permit to install a gas fireplace insert yourself, but a licensed gas fitter still has to make the gas line connection. You can do the box; you cannot do the gas.

Sources: City of Calgary — Homeowner permits and City of Calgary — Plumbing and gas permits

People assume gas rides along with plumbing. It doesn't. Gasfitting is a separate certification in Alberta — a Red Seal plumbing endorsement requires passing both the Plumber and the Gasfitter Class B interprovincial exams precisely because they're two different bodies of knowledge. How the permit and inspections actually go

This is the part people imagine as bureaucratic and it really isn't. Two visits, booked by you, at times you choose.

  1. Apply for the homeowner plumbing permit before you start. Applying after the fact is possible but it puts you in the position of uncovering work you already finished, which is exactly the expensive outcome the permit exists to prevent.
  2. Do the rough-in. All drainage, venting and water lines installed and properly supported, and the bathtub or shower valve in. Nothing gets covered yet — no drywall, no backfill, no insulation over the pipes.
  3. Book the rough-in inspection. Through 311 or the City's online inspection booking. Next-day requests generally need to be in by 2 p.m. Someone 18 or older has to be at the property to let the inspector in.
  4. Finish and book the final. All fixtures and equipment installed, any piping left for future fixtures sealed with approved plugs or caps, and hot water at fixtures not exceeding 49 °C — that last one catches DIYers who set a tank thermostat by feel.

Source: The City of Calgary — Homeowner electrical and plumbing permits

Failing an inspection is not a disaster. Inspectors tell you what's wrong, you fix it, they come back. The people who get hurt are the ones who never booked the first visit and buried a mistake in a wall.

The rental and secondary-suite question

The provincial exemption applies to property the owner intends "only for personal and not-for-profit use." On the plain meaning of those words, work in a unit you intend to rent out doesn't look like personal, not-for-profit use. The City's homeowner permit test points the same direction, and there it's unambiguous: the home cannot be a rental property.

We're presenting the provincial half as a reasonable reading of the wording, not settled law — we're not aware of a definitive published interpretation either way. If your project is heading toward a legal secondary suite, contact Alberta Apprenticeship and Industry Training or a lawyer and get it confirmed for your situation before you buy materials. This is the sort of thing that is cheap to ask about in advance and expensive to be wrong about later.

As of August 2026 that's where the question stands. Suites also bring in a separate technical requirement most renovators discover far too late: a normally-open backwater valve may only serve one dwelling unit, so one valve can't protect both the main house and the suite. What it actually costs you to skip the permit

Nothing, usually, for years. That's why so many people do it. The bill arrives at one of two moments.

When you sell

A permit leaves a record and no permit leaves nothing — which feels like an advantage right up until a buyer's inspector is standing in a bathroom that isn't on any permit. From there you're negotiating against your own renovation, and the discount they ask for is never $116.50.

When you claim

If a joint you made lets go, your insurer will want to know who did the work and whether it was inspected. One Alberta brokerage notes that clogs and damage from high water pressure are generally treated as maintenance and likely not covered at all. Don't guess at your own policy — ask your broker in writing now, while nothing is leaking.

Source: Acera Insurance — burst pipes and Alberta home insurance (brokerage commentary, not an insurer policy)

Where DIY genuinely makes sense

We install plumbing for a living and we still think you should do some of this yourself. It's your house and the money is yours.

  • Fixture swaps. Faucets, toilets, shower heads, supply lines, angle stops. No permit, well-documented, and the failure mode is a slow drip you catch in an hour.
  • Isolation and shut-offs. Knowing where your main valve is and being willing to use it is worth more than any repair skill. Note that the curb stop out front belongs to The City — that one is not yours to operate.
  • A permitted basement rough-in, if you're methodical. The inspections are the safety net. People do this successfully in Calgary all the time.

Where we'd push back: anything on gas, anything on the buried service line, and anything where a mistake floods finished space while you're at work.
