Your tank has stopped making hot water, and shortly somebody is going to ask whether you want to stay on gas or switch to electric. Worth knowing before that conversation: the two fuels break in different places, and in Calgary one costs about fifteen times as much per unit of heat as the other.

Short answer: an electric tank fails as an electrical part — almost always an element or thermostat — and the classic symptom is a short burst of hot water then cold, not no hot water at all. A gas tank fails as a combustion system: pilot, thermocouple, gas valve, or a vent that has quietly stopped drawing, which is why Alberta requires a carbon monoxide alarm in any home with a fuel-burning appliance. At published Calgary rates electricity costs $48.63 per gigajoule against natural gas at $2.12; allow for a gas tank losing heat up the flue and electric still runs roughly 15 times dearer. Repair the fuel you have.

Where each fuel actually breaks

Both tanks are the same steel vessel with the same glass lining, anode rod and relief valve. Everything below the water line is shared. What differs is the machinery putting heat in, and that's where the repair bills come from.

Only that last row decides whether you're buying a repair or a replacement, and it doesn't care what fuel you burn. If yours is leaking rather than just cold, go to what to check when there's no hot water and shut the water off first.

The distinction most pages get wrong: “a bit of hot water, then cold”

A residential electric tank has two elements, top and bottom, and they do not run together. The manual is explicit: “Only one element will come on at any one time. This is known as a flip/flop system.” The upper thermostat is in charge — it heats the top portion first, then hands power down to the lower thermostat for the rest.

Source: John Wood residential electric water heater manual, quoted directly.

That handover turns a symptom into a diagnosis, because the two failures look nothing alike:

  • Dead lower element. The top still heats, so you get hot water for about a third of a tank, then cold. People call this “we're running out of hot water,” assume the tank is finished, and live with it for months. It's usually one part.
  • Dead upper element or thermostat. The top never reaches temperature, so power never flips down. Nothing heats — no hot water at all, from a tank that is otherwise fine.

So on an electric tank, “we keep running out of hot water” is a fault code, not a capacity complaint. Have the elements checked before anyone quotes you a bigger tank — a far larger bill, and a wasted one.

Not a DIY test. A 4,500-watt element draws about 18.75 amps at 240 volts — our arithmetic from the manual's wattage table — and that circuit isn't safe just because the thermostat has switched off.

Why a gas failure asks a second question

When an element burns out, the worst outcome is a cold shower. A gas tank has a failure mode with no electric equivalent: the burner works perfectly and the vent stops doing its job. The manual is blunt — “never operate the water heater unless it is vented to the outdoors and has adequate air supply,” and “breathing carbon monoxide can cause brain damage or death.”

Which is why Alberta legislates it. Under the Alberta Building Code, certified carbon monoxide alarms are required in every building containing a residential occupancy that also contains a fuel-burning appliance and/or an attached storage garage — including service rooms holding those appliances, and within five metres of bedrooms. The provincial guidance names “back drafting chimneys and flues” as a source, singling out tightly sealed homes with ventilation fans.

Sources: John Wood residential gas water heater manual; Alberta Safety Codes Authority — carbon monoxide alarms (January 2020). These rules are provincial, not City — confirm what applies to your building with a certified gas fitter.

That last clause is a Calgary detail whether the document says so or not: furnace, water heater and range on one house, fans pulling air out, everything sealed hard for a −30 °C week. And on a side-wall power-vented unit, the outside terminal can be blocked by drifted snow or by ice from its own exhaust. Look at it after a heavy snow.

If a CO alarm sounds, that is not a plumbing call. Get everyone outside into fresh air, then phone the emergency number on your gas bill or 911 — not us, not any plumber. Our page on what counts as a plumbing emergency puts this above every water problem on it.

What Calgary's hard water does to each

The City's 2025 data puts the Bearspaw plant between 141 and 200 mg/L of calcium carbonate and Glenmore between 181 and 274. Divide by 17.1 for grains per gallon: roughly 8.2–11.7 gpg from Bearspaw, 10.6–16.0 from Glenmore. That conversion is ours; the City publishes only mg/L and doesn't say which plant serves which neighbourhood, so ask 311.

Source: The City of Calgary — water quality and hardness data (2025).

The usual local line is that hard water wrecks water heaters, full stop. Laboratory work says something more specific. A Battelle study for the Water Quality Association ran storage tanks on artificially hardened water:

  • On electric tanks the vessel survived; the element didn't. Efficiency held at the rated value “until sediment reached the bottom element and buried it, causing the bottom element to overheat.” Hard water “did not appear to impact the efficiency of electric storage water heaters or the lifetime of the tank” — and “cleaning the tank and replacing the lower element were found to restore full efficiency.”
  • On gas tanks the efficiency slid from 70.4 to 67.4 per cent over the equivalent of two years — three points nobody would notice on a bill, but real.

Source: Battelle (Paul et al., 2010) for the Water Quality Association, in PNNL-22921, filed with the California Energy Commission. Read the funding line: the WQA is the water-treatment trade body — we cite it because the electric finding cuts against its interest. Tests ran near 444 mg/L, above Calgary's 141–274: accelerated lab work, not a forecast.

That changes which maintenance job matters. On an electric tank, draining sediment protects the lower element from being buried — and if you already have the “short burst then cold” symptom, the study found cleaning the tank and replacing that element restored full performance. On a gas tank the sediment sits between burner and water instead, which is the rumbling people describe. Either way the anode rod decides the tank's life, and the manual is candid that “an anode can last from one to ten years”. More on hard water here.

The fuel-switch arithmetic, shown in full

Here is where the two Calgary pages competing for this question fall down. One says gas is cheaper “due to low natural gas prices in Alberta” and gives no figure. The other compares “$0.20 per cubic meter” of gas against “$0.12 per kilowatt-hour” and concludes gas saves “a couple hundred dollars a year.” Those aren't comparable units, and the conclusion is out by roughly a factor of four.

Before any efficiency argument, electricity costs 22.9 times as much per gigajoule of purchased energy in Calgary. Efficiency claws some back, because a gas tank sends part of its heat up the flue and an element doesn't. It doesn't come close to closing the gap.

Natural Resources Canada puts Alberta residential water heating at 42.5 petajoules across 1,601.0 thousand households in 2021 — 26.5 GJ per household a year, or $56 a year of gas. Whichever row you prefer, switching a Calgary gas tank to electric costs $700 to $920 more every year, indefinitely — two energy prices an order of magnitude apart, not a close call. The same method is why the tankless payback argument doesn't survive an Alberta gas bill either.

Where this is soft. The 26.5 GJ averages every Alberta dwelling, apartments and already-electric ones included, so a gas-heated detached home sits above it — widening the gap, not narrowing it.

If you're switching fuel anyway

Neither direction is a like-for-like swap, and both cost real money before you've heated anything.

  1. Gas to electric needs a dedicated 240-volt circuit, a panel with room for it — in an older Calgary home that can mean a service upgrade — and an electrical permit.
  2. Electric to gas needs gas supply, a vent to the outdoors and combustion air. You can't pull the gas permit yourself unless you hold Alberta first- or second-class gasfitter certification — the same rule that governs any gas line work.
  3. Either way the old fuel gets decommissioned. An abandoned gas line is capped, an abandoned circuit made safe. Ask for these as their own quote lines.

Sources: City of Calgary — homeowner permits; Alberta Tradesecrets — plumbing is a compulsory certification trade, gasfitting a separate ticket. The homeowner permit fee was $116.50 as of August 2026, rising every January 1. We could not re-open the City fee pages the day this was written — check with 311.

Against that running cost, the install difference barely registers. For most Calgary homes the right answer when a gas tank dies is another gas tank — and when an electric tank dies it's usually a part, not a tank. See replacement costs and water heater permits.
